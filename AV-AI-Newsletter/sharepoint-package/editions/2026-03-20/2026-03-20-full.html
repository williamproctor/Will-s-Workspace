Full Edition

This Week in AI

Fish Audio S2: Open-Source TTS That Lets You Direct Every Word

Fish Audio quietly released the most interesting voice AI tool we've seen in months. S2 is an open-source text-to-speech model that supports word-level emotion control — meaning you can tag individual words or phrases with natural language instructions like [excited], [somber], or [whispered], and the model adjusts its delivery at that exact moment.

It also does zero-shot voice cloning across 80 languages with sub-150ms latency. For our audio team, this is worth evaluating immediately. The word-level control changes TTS from "pick a voice and hope it works" to "direct the performance like you would with a human narrator." And it's fully open source.

Source: Fish Audio S2 announcement · ~March 10, 2026

Luma Agents Turn a $15M Campaign into $20K

Luma Labs launched Agents, powered by their multi-modal model Uni-1, which handles video, audio, image, and text generation end-to-end. The headline case study: a client converted a $15 million traditional advertising campaign into localized versions across markets in 40 hours for under $20,000.

That 750x cost reduction is eye-catching, even if the output quality isn't one-to-one with traditional production. What matters is the trajectory — this is the first credible example of AI creative agents operating across the full production pipeline, not just one modality. It's a direct competitor to ElevenLabs Flows (which we covered last week) and signals that the unified AI production platform is no longer speculative.

Source: Luma Labs announcement · ~March 11, 2026

ByteDance's Video Model Gets Cease-and-Desisted by Hollywood

The biggest cautionary tale of the week: ByteDance's AI video model Seedance 2.0 was hit with cease-and-desist letters from Disney, Warner Bros., Paramount, and Sony after users generated near-perfect reproductions of copyrighted characters and scenes. ByteDance shelved the model pending review.

This is the first major legal action against an AI video tool over character reproduction, and it's a wake-up call for any AV team using these tools. If your AI video output looks too much like someone else's IP, the legal risk is real. Expect every major video AI platform to accelerate content filtering in response.

Source: Tech press coverage of Seedance 2.0 takedown · ~March 11, 2026

Vidu Q3: The First AI Pipeline Built for Series Production

At SXSW, Vidu debuted Q3 — the first AI video pipeline designed specifically for animated series production. The key breakthrough: character consistency across episodes, automated lip-sync, and asset reuse. These are the exact problems that have kept AI video out of serialized content.

Every other AI video tool focuses on one-off generation. Q3 is built for recurring content — training series, internal communications, branded episodic content. If your team produces anything serialized, this is the tool to watch.

Source: Vidu Q3 SXSW debut · ~March 10, 2026

NVIDIA GTC: Vera Rubin and 10x Cheaper Inference

Jensen Huang's GTC keynote announced the Vera Rubin GPU architecture (successor to Blackwell), the OpenClaw robotics platform, and most importantly for us: 10x cheaper inference on current Blackwell hardware compared to Hopper. Huang positioned inference cost reduction — not raw training performance — as NVIDIA's priority.

Translation for AV teams: every AI-powered tool you use is about to get cheaper to run. Cloud rendering, voice synthesis, real-time captioning, video generation — the cost floor is dropping fast.

Source: NVIDIA GTC 2026 keynote · March 10, 2026

Voices This Week

Dylan Patel on Why GPUs Are Worth More, Not Less

Dylan Patel from SemiAnalysis laid out the three bottlenecks limiting AI compute scaling: lithography (ASML), memory (HBM), and power. The counterintuitive headline: "An H100 is worth more today than it was three years ago" — because newer, cheaper-to-run models like GPT-5.4 produce more value per GPU than older models ever could.

For AV teams, the practical takeaway is about vendor economics. Patel argued that Anthropic "screwed the pooch" compared to OpenAI on compute deals, which means Anthropic-powered tools may face capacity constraints that OpenAI-powered ones won't. If you're choosing between AI tool vendors, their compute infrastructure strategy matters. Also: $600 billion in combined Big Tech CapEx in 2026. The infrastructure build-out is real, and it will drive costs down for everyone downstream.

Julie Bedard on "AI Brain Fry"

BCG's Julie Bedard described a concept that should concern every team deploying AI tools at scale: "AI Brain Fry" — cognitive fatigue from overreliance on AI, where people stop thinking critically because the AI is handling so much of the cognitive load.

This isn't anti-AI sentiment. It's a human factors problem. If our team is using AI tools daily — and we should be — we need to design workflows that keep humans engaged and critical, not passive. The solution is better task design, not less AI.

Felix Rieseberg on Claude Cowork

Felix Rieseberg from Anthropic walked through Claude Cowork — a VM-based agent that's the user-friendly superset of Claude Code. The key quote: "Don't even write a memo, just build." Anthropic's internal culture has shifted to prototype-first, building all candidate products in parallel and picking winners.

What matters for AV teams: Cowork can organize files, upload videos, automate browser workflows, and handle taxes (yes, really). The "skills" system — lightweight markdown instruction files — means you can teach the agent new capabilities without writing code. Imagine a skill file that says "when a video lands in the review folder, generate a transcript and draft clips." That's where this is heading.

Simon Last on the 1,000X Engineer

Simon Last from Notion made a bold claim: "You can be a 100 or 1,000X engineer if you use the tools right now." The implication for AV teams is direct — individuals with the right AI toolchain can produce output that previously required entire teams.

Common Threads

The AI Production Platform Race Is On

Three major launches this week — Fish Audio S2, Luma Agents/Uni-1, and Hume AI TADA — join last week's ElevenLabs Flows in the race to become the unified AI production environment. We've gone from zero credible multi-modal production tools to four in two weeks. None of them are ready to replace traditional production pipelines today, but the direction is unmistakable: the industry is converging on end-to-end AI creative platforms that handle video, audio, voice, and text in a single workflow.

What this means for us: Start experimenting with at least one of these platforms now. The learning curve matters more than the specific tool choice — the skills transfer. We recommend Fish Audio S2 for audio teams (open source, word-level control) and keeping an eye on Luma Agents for full-pipeline experimentation.

Copyright Is the New Frontier Risk for AI Video

The Seedance 2.0 takedown, combined with expanding EU/US deepfake regulations and the Grammarly identity incident, makes one thing clear: intellectual property is the biggest unresolved risk in AI content generation. Studios are willing to sue. Regulators are advancing mandatory labeling. And platforms are scrambling to add content filters after the fact.

What this means for us: We need an internal policy on AI-generated content provenance before we need one, not after. Any content produced with AI video tools should be documented: which tool, which prompts, which reference materials. This isn't bureaucracy — it's insurance.

"AI Brain Fry" Should Shape Our Adoption Strategy

Julie Bedard's concept of cognitive fatigue from AI overuse deserves attention beyond just awareness. If heavy AI users are experiencing reduced critical thinking and over-delegation, our tool rollout strategy needs to account for it. The answer isn't slowing adoption — it's designing workflows where humans remain engaged decision-makers, not just prompt writers. Pair AI tools with explicit review checkpoints. Rotate between AI-assisted and manual work. And watch for signs of over-reliance.

For AV teams, this means you could automate complex workflows (ingest → transcribe → timestamp → clip) by writing a text file, not building an integration.
