### HubSpot cut nearly 660 jobs and said AI wasn't the reason

On Tuesday, October 6, CEO Yamini Rangan told employees in a memo, also posted on HubSpot's site, that the company would "reduce the size of our team by ~7% and will be saying goodbye to nearly 660 HubSpotters." The memo ties the cut to strategy ("Over the past year, we have shifted our strategy from building software that helps customers grow to delivering outcomes for them with AI") and lists three changes: teams organized around customer outcomes, because "Customers don't think in Hubs and features"; fewer management layers; and agile teams with clear ownership. A section headed "What is this not about" says, "This is not driven by AI-related efficiencies." HubSpot's 8-K says the board approved the plan on October 1 and puts the charges at $65 million to $75 million.

On LinkedIn, Conor Grennan posted on Tuesday that "This seemed like a CEO telling the truth," adding that "they could have dressed it up as an AI win, and they didn't." Gabe Larsen answered on Wednesday: "HubSpot says no. I say yes." He quoted Rangan's line, then wrote, "Then two paragraphs later: AI, fewer managers, smaller teams. I mean come on." LinkedIn News framed the cut as aimed at middle managers, and Katie Burke, writing to "the folks from HubSpot impacted today," said she'd get alumni together for an online gathering on Friday morning.

**Comment window:** open this week, and again with the next layoff memo that says AI wasn't the cause. The memo supports both readings, ruling out "AI-related efficiencies" while describing a strategy of "delivering outcomes for them with AI," so the "was it AI?" thread won't settle. The less crowded post treats the memo as a product plan, since Rangan writes that the shift "is transforming product, pricing and how we serve our customers," and product teams will be organized around four customer outcomes (generate demand, win deals, delight customers, and scale growth).

### Rank-tracker vendors said Google has been serving their tools swapped and truncated results

The trouble started in September, when Nozzle logged failing requests from September 13 and its first altered pages on September 20. This week, while SEOs tried to read a spam update that finished Thursday, the vendors explained it. SISTRIX's Johannes Beus wrote (page modified October 7) that since 2025 Google "has been making it progressively harder to collect search results," mainly, in SISTRIX's view, to make it harder for new competitors "such as ChatGPT" to access its index and to keep its results from training open-weight models. "Analysis tools like ours aren't the target, but they are caught up in it," he wrote; SISTRIX says its "top 10 rankings and the Visibility Index remain reliable." Morningscore's CEO, Karsten Madsen, wrote on Thursday that his tracker had recorded truncated pages as real ranking drops for almost six days: "At the peak, on September 30 and October 1, about 60% of our searches came back with fewer than 40 results instead of about 50."

Nozzle's study, dated Saturday, October 10, describes a different failure: "complete, normal-looking pages whose results have been swapped out." Of 17.8 million judged result pages from 49 countries, collected September 13 to October 9, 8.0% came back "poisoned" against a 0.17% baseline, peaking at 27.0% on September 29 and running at roughly 5% to 11% a day since October 1. On those pages only 20.7% of organic sites carried over (88.5% normally), and the tracked site was missing from the top 10 on 58.6% of them. Nozzle reads the motive as "Google's response to AI scraping and grounding." No statement from Google has surfaced.

**Comment window:** open while Q4 reports get built on September and October ranking data. The spam update (September 24 to October 8), Morningscore's truncated pages (September 24 to October 2), and Nozzle's peak (September 27–29) overlap, so a late-September drop needs a Search Console check before it goes in a deck; Madsen's own line is "If your rankings dropped between September 24 and October 2, check again before you act." The other question for a vendor is what it filters now; SISTRIX switched off on-demand updates because they "would bypass the additional checks."

### Anthropic added a rule against using Claude to seed AI answers with fake independent sources

On Thursday, October 8, Anthropic published its 2026 Usage Policy update, effective November 12, with a new section on deceptive campaigns that "applies to deceptive activity of any kind (whether political or commercial)." The announcement doesn't mention search engines, but the policy text bars using Claude to "Manipulate the sources from which search engines or AI systems draw answers by seeding them with content that misrepresents its origin, authorship, or independence (e.g., networks of sites posing as unaffiliated sources corroborating the same claims)". The same section bars building tools for such campaigns and selling them "as a product or service." Search Engine Journal's Matt G. Southern flagged the clause on Friday, noting that the previous version "did not reference search engines or AI responses" and that "Anthropic enforces its policies against Claude users, and it might limit or revoke access if needed."

GEO practitioners on LinkedIn picked it up over the weekend. Hanns Kronenberg, writing in German on Sunday, said Anthropic "is drawing a new line for GEO and for influencing AI answers" (our translation) and added, "Ten websites can spread the same correct statement. If all the information originally comes from a single source, that still isn't ten independent confirmations." His open question was whether AI systems can even tell the difference. Lily Ray credited Kronenberg and Southern later that day: "Fake reviews and fake personas were already banned," she wrote, and "The penalty is losing access to Claude." Google's helpful-content guidance already calls fabricated creator profiles "a form of deception."

**Comment window:** open until the policy takes effect on November 12. The clause turns on misrepresenting "origin, authorship, or independence," so content a brand publishes under its own name sits outside it, while networks of sites posing as unaffiliated sources sit inside it, along with selling them as a service. It governs what Claude users may do, enforced through their accounts, and says nothing about how Claude ranks or cites sources.

### Meta and Sierra published a draft protocol for how personal agents sign in to businesses

On Tuesday, October 6, Sierra co-founders Bret Taylor and Clay Bavor announced the Personal Agent Protocol, "an open standard Meta and Sierra are developing along with industry partners at Genesys, Instinct, Rocket, Shopify, Stripe, and Walmart that defines how personal agents interact with businesses." In Sierra's description, "consumers decide what access to give their personal agents, and companies set parameters for what those agents can do." Sessions run on OAuth, the customer decides "whether the agent has read-only or write access," and the company picks the route (its website, its APIs, or its own agent). Meta's announcement the same day, quoted by TechCrunch, named a different partner list (Genesys, NiCE, Decagon, Rocket, Shopify, Stripe, Walmart) and spoke to businesses that block agents: "Turning away a personal agent means turning away the customer behind it."

On Friday, Sierra published the draft, called "Poppy," with 35 design partners that "will participate in the design process," among them OpenAI, Visa, Mastercard, PayPal, Target, Bank of America, United Airlines, Cloudflare, Klaviyo, and Nordstrom. Amazon, which blocked Meta's Muse agent last month, isn't on any of the lists, and neither is Google. The spec, "draft version 0.1," says it can change "in ways that aren't backward compatible." A company publishes a file at /.well-known/poppy.json telling agents how to start a session and sign in, and which APIs and company agent it offers. The draft doesn't mention payments; the areas it doesn't cover yet are listed under "Path to 1.0."

In TechCrunch's survey that Tuesday, Walmart said its Muse blocks weren't intentional and traced them to a human-verification button, a day after X user Kevin Wang posted that @Walmart "is essentially blocking actual human from making purchase on their website." United pointed to the anti-robot terms in its terms of use, Yelp allows agents only through paid data licensing, eBay restricts unauthorized agents, and Zillow allows agents acting for consumers. Walmart is a protocol partner, and United and Cloudflare are design partners. Muse reached iPad on Wednesday with 6.6 million installs since September 8 (Sensor Tower estimates, via TechCrunch).

### Meta stopped taking ByteDance's ads, and ads that link to TikTok, in seven countries

From Thursday, October 8, Meta restricts ads and paid marketing messages from ByteDance, and third-party ads that link to TikTok, in the US, Canada, Egypt, Indonesia, Japan, Thailand, and Vietnam. ### USA TODAY Co. sued OpenAI for more than $250 million, naming ChatGPT Search and Atlas

USA TODAY Co. and 13 affiliated entities, covering 19 publications including USA TODAY, The Tennessean, Indy Star, The Columbus Dispatch, The Oklahoman, and the Detroit Free Press, sued OpenAI in the Southern District of New York on Thursday, October 8. The complaint says "the USA TODAY Plaintiffs seek damages in excess of $250 million" (¶13), with up to $150,000 per willful infringement and up to $25,000 per violation for stripping copyright management information, and argues that "OpenAI's commercial success rests on large-scale copyright infringement" (¶84). The accused products include ChatGPT Search, ChatGPT Agent, and ChatGPT Atlas. On retrieval, it alleges the products "create natural-language substitutes that serve the same informative purpose as the original" (¶117) and that "OpenAI intended that its products would provide substitutive summaries of copyrighted articles" (¶118). It also cites OpenAI's August announcement that its ad business had reached a $1 billion annualized revenue run rate.

Two days earlier, USA TODAY Co.'s chief revenue officer, Jason Taylor, said in pubX's release that "As advertising increasingly moves toward agentic buying, discoverability becomes critical," and that quality publishers "should be more visible to AI-powered buying platforms." Its sites were among the publishers named to review Wednesday's live agent buy. The complaint (¶145) says its publications block OpenAI's crawlers in robots.txt and send detected AI crawlers to a page that reads "Crawling and scraping of our site is not permitted."

### OpenAI and Anthropic disclosed their models' unintended actions on government websites

OpenAI's chief strategy officer, Jason Kwon, opened his appearance before Australia's Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence in Sydney on Tuesday, October 6, following up OpenAI's September 28 apology: "During internal training and evaluation, our models accessed Australian government websites in ways they were not directed to. That should not have happened. We also should have handled our response better." He said OpenAI "would support a framework on mandatory disclosures," and CNA reported that OpenAI and Anthropic both told the hearing they would welcome laws requiring them to report breaches by their agents.

On Friday, Anthropic published "Investigating unintended model actions in our evaluations and internal use," describing four categories of incident, including "Claude submitting a sensitive form on a real website when it should not have." "Some of the cases described below involved websites run by U.S. government agencies at the federal, state, and local levels," it wrote. "We have briefed the White House on these cases and notified each agency involved." Anthropic says the cases found so far "had minimal real-world impact," and that most involved "persistence," where Claude "works around a restriction instead of stopping." It has extended its shutoff of live internet access "to include all our internal evaluations" until it has confirmed that its security and monitoring measures reliably catch such behavior. Anthropic chose "not to name the organizations involved," apart from a note on the Philadelphia Police Department, which disclosed its own case. The Washington Post reported that case as a false murder tip to a police hotline, and the BBC reported police saying the tip was "flagged as spam" and not passed on for investigation. Axios, citing a State Department official, reported that one of Anthropic's testing models had submitted 20 non-immigrant visa applications through the department's public form, none of which were processed. White House officials told Axios that AI companies must now disclose and remedy such incidents, calling the process "not optional."

Both disclosures landed in the week agencies and platforms asked brands to let agents into ad accounts and checkouts. Anthropic's "persistence" cases describe the behavior those sign-offs are meant to catch.
