### Kalshi's AI ad copied a YouTuber's video and swapped his face for a white man's

On Wednesday, September 23, Elliot Choy posted two stills side by side on X. One came from his September 2025 video about moving into a New York apartment. The other was a Kalshi ad that closely copied it, down to the computer and the books in the background, with a white man where Choy had been: "i guess kalshi saw this and thought they should steal my video, turn me into a white dude, and run it as an ad." He found it because YouTube served it to him, per Futurism, and NPR's Bobby Allyn confirmed the same day that the ad was real. Kalshi told Business Insider the ad was about four months old and made by an agency that supplied the video, said it no longer uses AI-generated content depicting people, and declined to name the agency; a rep told Allyn that Kalshi is "reviewing our relationship with" it. Choy wasn't the first. Toronto creator Pushpek Sidhu posted in June that a Kalshi ad had replicated his World Cup video, script and gestures included, with a white man in his place. "They could easily pay me a little bit," he told BI.

Two weeks earlier, on September 9, Kalshi's head of growth, Keaton Inglis, posted on LinkedIn that Kalshi had been "the first brand to run a 100% AI-generated ad on broadcast" and had since "pivoted in the opposite direction: all-in on unmistakably human creativity." By Kalshi's account, the ad Choy found was made months before that post, and it was still being served two weeks after it.

**Comment window:** open. The angle to post: "the agency made it" left the blame with Kalshi, because Kalshi is the name in Choy's post. If a vendor can generate a person, the contract has to say whose footage and whose face it may use, and licensing is the cheap option; Mick Feldman of the influencer agency The Now told BI that licensing an existing creator video typically costs about a quarter of making a new one.

### Google switched on AI Mode checkout for Shopify stores, and Google Analytics won't see the orders

The email started landing on Tuesday, September 22: "Your Shopify store was matched to your Merchant Center, enabling native checkout on Google AI Mode and Gemini." The next lines settled who had opted in: "Eligible products are automatically included. There's nothing for you to set up." Menachem Ani, founder of JXT, posted his on X, and when Smarter Ecommerce's Mike Ryan asked whether anything had to be switched on, Ani answered, "No action on our end." Nic McDonough posted the checkout live that morning. Google has run checkout on its Universal Commerce Protocol for a limited set of merchants since February and widened it on Friday, September 18; per Search Engine Land, the Shopify version covers US merchants selling to US customers with a valid Merchant Center account. For Shopify stores the switch sits under Sales channels > Agentic; turning it off keeps products discoverable in AI Mode and Gemini and sends buyers to the store's own checkout.

On reporting, Shopify's help page says, "Google Analytics and custom pixels won't fire in Google AI Mode and Gemini's direct checkout." Only two server-to-server events come through, checkout started and checkout completed. Orders land in the Shopify admin with channel attribution, so the revenue shows up in Shopify and never reaches GA4 or the store's client-side pixels. The program behind it, Shopify's agentic storefronts, is "active by default for eligible stores" across ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, and Meta surfaces such as Muse as well.

**Comment window:** open through holiday code freezes. Default-on is how the AI checkout race is being run, so the useful post is an audit: which AI channels have direct checkout switched on for each store, and where those orders show up in reporting.

### AI Overviews started showing more links, and some of them open AI Mode

On Monday, September 21, Malte Landwehr of Peec AI, an AI search analytics company, posted a chart on X: "Google massively increased the number of external links within AI Overview answers." In Peec's sample, the share of AI Overviews with an external link inside the answer text sat near zero from August 25, stepped up on September 11, and reached 26.2% on September 20. Earlier the same day, Gagan Ghotra posted an 18-second recording of a different link: bold, underlined text inside an AI Overview that opened a query in AI Mode, with no website at the other end. "Another day – Another Tactic! from Google to push users from usual search results to AI Mode," he wrote. Barry Schwartz shared Peec's chart with a caveat ("This doesn't 'feel' right to me") and a disclosure of his small investment in the company.

Peec's chart counts links in answers and doesn't publish its sample size, markets, or devices. Landwehr's guess, in the replies, is that "over time more and more links will become links redirecting users to AI Mode."

**Comment window:** open while the chart circulates. The practical post: a link count is inventory, so check where the links go before reporting a win. An AI Mode link keeps the searcher inside Google and may never register in Search Console.

## Act Two — Lead story

The most important hard-news story this week is **Amazon locked Muse out; Shopify, PayPal, and Expedia plugged it in**. Give it the most discussion time.

Meta launched Muse on September 8 as a personal agent with its own browser, and it caught on fast. It overtook ChatGPT as the top free iPhone app in the US on Friday, September 18, and Sensor Tower counted more than 2.5 million downloads by Monday, per CNBC (in their own first 13 days, ChatGPT had 3.1 million and Claude 400,000). Meta's shares rose more than 11% that Monday. On Sunday night, September 20, Amazon started showing Muse users a popup: "Continued access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon's Conditions of Use, to which our customers have agreed." Amazon's complaints, per GeekWire and Adweek, are that Meta never told it Muse would shop there, the agent doesn't identify itself, it appears to capture and store customer credentials, and it skips the personalization Amazon's pages are built on. Amazon asked Meta to "remove Amazon from the experience"; Meta says Muse "has no visibility into people's passwords or payment methods."

The popup cites Amazon's Conditions of Use and makes no hacking claim. Amazon lost its preliminary injunction against Perplexity's Comet browser at the Ninth Circuit on August 4, in a ruling that the user, not the AI company, is the one accessing Amazon's computers, and GeekWire notes the ruling left claims built on contracts and terms of service open. Amazon's rule, as it put it in both of GeekWire's stories this week, is that agents should identify themselves and let the site decide whether to take part; its own Buy for Me agent works that way and lets brands opt out.

Shopify's Tobi Lütke announced on Monday that Muse can check out with Shop Pay "on all Shopify stores," and PayPal and Expedia signed on Tuesday. The same Tuesday, six banks (ASB, Bank of America, Capital One, Commonwealth Bank of Australia, ING, and NatWest) published shared principles for agent payments covering transparency, safety, privacy and data, choice, and interoperability, with implementation details promised in a second paper. On Wednesday, at its Accelerate conference, Amazon launched a plugin that lets sellers run their Amazon business from Anthropic's Claude or Amazon's Quick assistant, with the seller choosing which data it reaches and approving each action before it happens.

For brands, reach through shopping agents now depends on where they sell. Shopify says its agentic storefronts, which cover Meta surfaces such as Muse, are active by default for eligible stores, while Muse can't buy on Amazon at all. Forbes' Maureen Kerr pointed out on Saturday that Meta keeps similar control over rival AI assistants on WhatsApp, so neither company in this fight is arguing for open access in general.

### ChatGPT's share of AI prompts fell from 70% to 50% in six months

Comscore's Q2 AI Intelligence Report, out Tuesday, September 22, measured prompt volume across its panel. ChatGPT's share fell from 70% in January to 50% in June; Gemini's rose from 17% to 30%, and Claude's from 2% to 11%. ChatGPT is still growing (168 million desktop conversations in June, and 99 million multiplatform visitors against 59 million a year earlier), so this is a bigger market split more ways. Reach kept climbing too: 35% of desktop users and 29% of mobile users visited an AI assistant in June, up from 25% and 12% in January 2025. On the search side, the share of Google desktop searches showing an AI Overview rose from 25.8% in July 2025 to 39.4% in June, and Bing's Copilot Search went from 11.8% to 17.3%. Paid placements are arriving inside answers: sponsored ads appeared in 6% of hotel-related AI prompts with source links in March and 24% by May.

"Brands and publishers can no longer assume that a single AI experience defines the market," said Smriti Sharma, Comscore's chief analytics and technology officer. For AEO programs, a visibility tracker weighted toward ChatGPT is now watching about half the prompts in Comscore's panel, while Gemini's share nearly doubled in six months.

### YouTube is putting brand deals inside Studio and likeness alerts on phones

At Made on YouTube on Wednesday, September 23, Neal Mohan pitched Studio as the place creators will land brand deals: "YouTube Creator Partnerships helps creators get noticed by brands, while Ask Studio will soon make it easy to craft tailored responses to briefs, build proactive pitch materials, and land deals faster." The affiliate program expands to 35 countries by year-end, with Amazon supported internationally and product tags that localize offers to where the viewer is. Creators have run more than 40 million title and thumbnail A/B tests since 2024, per YouTube's Aparna Pappu; Studio now offers dynamic thumbnails that serve the best of three options by audience segment, and testing up to three cuts of the video itself, to see which hook holds attention, is coming soon. Viewers will be able to build home feeds from prompts ("Witty podcasts for my daily 30-minute commute"), and Ask YouTube is expanding into a product recommendation guide.

Likeness detection is coming to the mobile app, and speaking-voice detection joins facial matching later this year. Pappu also cited YouTube's first GenAI trends report: "72% of U.S. video posters ages 14-44 have already used AI to help them create or edit in the past year." Once Ask Studio's brief tools ship, a creator's reply to a brand brief may be drafted with a model's help, which makes the brief itself model input. Prompt-built feeds and a shopping guide inside Ask YouTube also pull discovery on YouTube closer to search, where titles, descriptions, and product tags do more of the work.

### Google promised a report that shows what AI Max chose; Microsoft's is already in its API

Google said on Wednesday, September 23, that it's building a single AI Max report showing "what search terms triggered your ads, which creative assets the user saw, and exactly where they landed on your website," with details and availability "later this year." The same post opened the AI Brief closed beta, where advertisers steer AI Max in their own words, to Dutch, French, German, Italian, Japanese, Portuguese, and Spanish. Parts of that view exist today: the Google Ads API's AI Max combination view pairs the query with the headline and landing page, and the search terms report has a "Search terms and landing pages from AI Max" option. The new report would put the whole journey in one place, and it's coming while Google moves campaigns that use Dynamic Search Ads, automatically created assets, or campaign-level broad match onto AI Max automatically, which its help page says starts this month.

Microsoft's version is live for anyone on its API. The Search Term Landing Page report ties a query to the headline that served and the final URL that got the click, and Performance Max campaigns can exclude Computers, Smartphones, or Tablets at the campaign level (at least one category has to stay eligible). Trade coverage arrived on September 24 and 25, but Microsoft's release notes list both under August, so this is catch-up coverage of shipped features. PPC leads will spend the quarter explaining what the automation did with the budget, and both platforms now offer the same kind of evidence, a view that runs from query to landing page.

### The UK wants AI assistants on Google's choice screens

The Competition and Markets Authority published strengthened proposals on Wednesday, September 23. Google would have to offer Android and Chrome users a choice of search service when they first set up the phone or open Chrome, then prompt them every year to pick a default. AI assistants that meet technical and security criteria could appear on those screens, and any provider on them would have to "fairly attribute publisher content when used." The CMA cited "the growing use of new services like ChatGPT and Perplexity," and chief executive Sarah Cardell said AI assistants are services "people are increasingly using as their search service, but aren't included on Google's choice screens." Comments close October 9, with a final decision expected by year-end. The proposal builds on the publisher opt-out from Google's AI features that the CMA imposed on June 3.

For UK campaigns, a yearly default prompt means search share can shift every year, and an assistant that wins the default on an Android phone brings its own ad products and citation rules with it.

### Temu pulled its partnership ads from likely fake creators within days of Fortune's story

Fortune reported on Friday, September 25, that Temu largely stopped funding a network of likely fake creator accounts on Meta after Fortune's August 31 story. Online Risk Labs, a Czech nonprofit, had estimated that Temu spent up to $962 million over 16 months on partnership ads (creator posts a brand pays to boost) in the UK and EU, and judged about 73 of the 100 accounts carrying the most Temu ads to be probably fake, citing repeated handle changes and bases in Russia and China despite European targeting. By September 4, 90 of those accounts showed no Temu ads, and Temu's weekly partnership ads fell from 115,114 to 64,008. Total weekly Temu ads fell only from about 167,000 to 147,000 over the same stretch, per Shopifreaks' reading of Fortune's figures, so ads outside the partnership format rose. Neither Temu nor Meta commented.

Meta has said partnership ads are on course for a $10 billion annual run rate. The format sells a creator's identity as the trust signal, and ORL's judgment rests on account signals like name changes and location, which any brand can check before boosting creator content at scale.
