This Week in AI

Blender Camera References Turn AI Video Into a Controlled Shot

The most useful AI video trend this week is not a single product release. It is a production pattern: build the camera move in a 3D tool first, then use that motion as the control layer for generation.

The workflow shows up in a few different forms:

  • A Blender camera path or animation is rendered as a low-detail reference video.
  • Blender depth, normal, outline, or pose passes are exported frame by frame.
  • A grey-box scene provides the camera move, perspective, occlusion, and basic blocking.
  • ComfyUI or a similar pipeline feeds those passes into a video model as control guidance.
  • The model generates the final look while the 3D reference preserves the intended motion and layout.

RunComfy's Blender to ComfyUI AI Renderer 2.0 is a clear example. It reads structure from depth, outline, or pose passes and uses that to drive Wan VACE video generation. The key phrase in the documentation is that the final footage stays aligned with the "original animation intent." In practical terms, Blender supplies the camera and scene geometry; the video model supplies the finished visual treatment.

Other technical projects point in the same direction. ComfyUI Blender temporal nodes load Blender EXR depth and normal passes as ControlNet conditioning, treating Blender's Z-depth as ground-truth scene data rather than estimating depth from an already-generated video. LooseControlVideo, a June research release, uses spatial blocking with oriented 3D boxes to direct object placement and motion through a VACE-style conditioning path. VerseCrafter-style systems also frame the problem as 4D control: camera trajectory, object motion, and scene structure coordinated over time.

The AV relevance is straightforward. Text prompts are weak at exact camera language. "Slow dolly in" can mean many things. A camera path in Blender is specific: frame count, focal length, easing, distance, parallax, and subject position are all defined. The AI model no longer has to infer the camera move from language alone.

This is not yet a push-button production pipeline. It still requires 3D setup, pass export, model-specific control workflows, and careful review. But the direction is important: previs is becoming an input to generation. The rough 3D scene is no longer only a planning artifact; it can become the control track the AI follows.

Seedance 2.5 Preview: Longer Clips, More References, More Control

ByteDance previewed Seedance 2.5 on June 23 at its Volcano Engine FORCE conference in Beijing. The headline claim is a single native 30-second video clip, generated directly rather than stitched together from shorter clips.

The other major claim is reference capacity. Coverage from The Next Web and several follow-up explainers reported support for up to 50 multimodal reference inputs, including images, audio clips, 3D white-box models, and style references. That is a very different control surface from a single prompt or a few still images.

The preview also described:

  • Native 4K output and 10-bit color depth.
  • Around 20 percent better prompt adherence, according to ByteDance.
  • Audio co-processed with visual signals rather than added separately.
  • Region-level editing that changes part of a frame while preserving the rest.
  • Low-detail 3D preview controls before committing to a full render.

The important caveat: Seedance 2.5 is not broadly available yet. It is in enterprise beta, with a public launch targeted for early July 2026. There are no independent 2.5 benchmark results yet. The shipping Seedance 2.0 model has performed strongly on independent video leaderboards, but 2.5 still needs public testing before its claims can be treated as measured results.

Still, the direction is notable. Seedance 2.5 appears to be aimed less at quick prompt clips and more at controlled production inputs: references, 3D blocking, longer duration, local edits, synchronized audio, and fewer disconnected generation steps.

That connects directly to the Blender trend. The question for AI video is shifting from "Can it generate a convincing shot?" to "Can it follow the shot we planned?" Seedance 2.5's preview suggests ByteDance is making that the center of the product.

GPT-5.6: The Benchmark Story Is Long-Horizon Agent Work

OpenAI announced the GPT-5.6 series on June 26 in limited preview: Sol as the flagship model, Terra as the balanced model, and Luna as the fast, lower-cost model.

The rollout is restricted at first to selected trusted partners through the API and Codex, with broader availability planned in the coming weeks. OpenAI says the limited release is part of a phased safety and coordination process around higher-capability models.

The pricing structure is also clearer than the access timeline:

  • GPT-5.6 Sol: $5 input / $30 output per 1M tokens
  • GPT-5.6 Terra: $2.50 input / $15 output per 1M tokens
  • GPT-5.6 Luna: $1 input / $6 output per 1M tokens

The benchmark story is not ordinary chat quality. It is agentic work: command-line workflows, long-running coding tasks, cybersecurity evaluations, and scientific analysis.

OpenAI says GPT-5.6 Sol sets a new state of the art on Terminal-Bench 2.1, a benchmark focused on command-line tasks that require planning, iteration, and tool coordination. VentureBeat reported the specific OpenAI-provided figures: Sol reached 91.91% in ultra mode and 88.76% in max mode, compared with GPT-5.5 at 83.4% and Claude Mythos 5 at 88%. VentureBeat also reported that Sol cleared 50.9% in code mode on Agent's Last Exam, the first model in that reporting to cross the halfway mark.

The new model family also introduces two inference modes worth noting:

  • max gives Sol more time to reason deeply.
  • ultra uses subagents to divide up complex work.

For AV teams, GPT-5.6 matters mostly in the tooling layer around production: scripts, internal apps, file organization, automations, QA passes, publishing workflows, and agents that need to operate across project context. It is not an AV generator. It is another sign that frontier-model competition is moving toward systems that can plan, use tools, and finish multi-step work with fewer human restarts.

Sonilo Brings Video-to-Music Generation to fal.ai

Sonilo announced on June 22 that its licensed video-to-music model is now available on fal.ai.

The model analyzes a video and generates an original soundtrack matched to the clip's duration, pacing, and emotional arc. The generated music is delivered as a separate audio track, so editors can adjust it independently from dialogue, voiceover, or existing sound effects. Through fal.ai, Sonilo says the model can score videos up to 600 seconds long, and API access is available for developers building media tools.

The useful detail is that the model is video-conditioned. Instead of asking for "calm background music" in the abstract, the video itself becomes the timing reference. That makes this category different from general prompt-to-music systems. It is closer to automated scoring: the picture drives the structure.

This fits the week's broader pattern. Video models are taking 3D camera references and control passes. Music models are taking the actual video timeline as input. The control surface is moving away from text alone and toward the media objects production teams already use.

Common Threads

Text Prompts Are Becoming the Least Precise Control

The strongest video workflows this week all use something more specific than text: Blender camera paths, depth passes, 3D blockouts, reference images, audio clips, regional masks, and timeline-conditioned music. Text is still part of the system, but it is no longer the only instruction.

Reference Capacity Is Becoming a Competitive Feature

Seedance 2.5's preview claim of up to 50 multimodal references is important because it shows where model competition is going. A production team rarely has one piece of context. It has a shot list, style frames, reference images, audio timing, prior cuts, brand constraints, and sometimes a 3D scene. Models that can accept more of that context can be directed more precisely.

Agent Benchmarks Are Moving Toward Real Work

GPT-5.6's most interesting benchmark claims are not single-answer tests. Terminal-Bench and Agent's Last Exam measure tool use, planning, iteration, and task completion. That is closer to the way AI is being used around AV production: not as a one-shot answer, but as a helper moving through folders, scripts, metadata, reviews, and publishing steps.

Novel Ideas Worth Watching

Grey-Box First, Generate Second

The old AI video workflow started with a prompt. The emerging workflow starts with a rough scene: boxes, camera path, depth, pose, timing, and maybe a first frame. That rough scene does not need to look finished. It needs to be spatially correct. The finished image can come later.

Region-Level Video Editing

Seedance 2.5 and Aleph 2.0 both point toward a future where editing a generated video is less about regenerating the whole clip and more about changing a specific region, subject, frame, or object while preserving the rest. That is much closer to how editors think about revision.

Video-Conditioned Audio

Sonilo's fal.ai launch is a reminder that audio generation is moving toward picture-aware systems. Instead of generating a generic track and cutting it down, the model can read the video timing first and compose around it.

Tip of the Week

Back to Basics, Week 5: Ask for a Delta

Last week, the Back to Basics series covered Tell It What Not to Change: give the model preservation rules before asking it to revise something.

This week builds on that idea: ask for a delta.

A delta is a short list of what changed. It is useful because AI outputs can look polished even when they quietly changed something important. If you ask for a delta, the model has to report its own edits back to you.

Use it after any revision task:

Revise this production note for clarity.

Preserve exactly:
- Speaker names
- Timecodes
- Technical terms
- Approved wording

After the revised version, include a delta:
- What changed
- What stayed the same
- Anything that still needs human review

For AV work, this is useful with scripts, captions, show notes, edit notes, equipment summaries, SharePoint copy, and documentation. It gives the reviewer a map of the changes instead of forcing them to compare every line from scratch.

The small habit: whenever you ask AI to revise, add one sentence at the end:

After the revision, list the exact changes you made.

That one line often makes the output easier to trust because it separates the revised text from the review trail.
