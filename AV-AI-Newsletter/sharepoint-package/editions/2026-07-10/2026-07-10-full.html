This Week in AI

Uber’s Agentic Pods Move AI Adoption From Individual Tasks to Departmental Workflows

Uber CTO Praveen Neppalli Naga published one of the most concrete large-organization AI adoption reports so far.

The headline figures are substantial. According to Naga:

  • 99 percent of Uber engineers use AI tools.
  • More than 70 percent of pull requests are attributed to local or cloud agents.
  • Uber engineers have created more than 2,500 agent skills across the software development lifecycle.

Those figures are company-reported and have not been independently audited, but the more useful part of the story is the operating model Uber developed after engineering adoption became widespread.

Uber wanted to move agentic AI into finance, legal, operations, marketing, customer support, HR, procurement, and other business functions. Its answer was a program called Agentic Pods.

The company selected approximately 30 AI-proficient engineers with deep knowledge of Uber’s systems and paired each one with a domain expert from a business function. Every pod received two weeks:

  • Days 1–2: Shadow the expert, observe each step, document the workflow, and ask questions.
  • Day 3: Prioritize opportunities based on scale, repetition, business impact, and data availability.
  • Days 4–5: Build a working agent alongside the person doing the job.
  • Days 6–9: Validate it with additional people performing the same work.
  • Day 10: Ship.

Naga said Uber ran 16 pods across 16 business functions in two months. He reported several examples:

  • Capital allocation analysis across 150 cities fell from 15 hours to 30 minutes.
  • Financial pacing reports fell from two days to 10 minutes.
  • Marketing website quality assurance fell from two weeks to 50 minutes.
  • A support operation with 9,000 manually created workflows moved to self-service automation.

The larger finding was not simply faster task completion. Naga said the biggest gains came from redesigning the workflow around AI: removing handoffs, reducing unnecessary approvals, replacing older tools, and connecting work across systems and teams.

That distinction is relevant to any department evaluating AI. A task can often be automated from a written instruction. A workflow is harder. It includes exceptions, judgment, file locations, approval patterns, unwritten conventions, and the practical knowledge held by the people doing the work. Uber’s model places the technical builder beside the domain expert long enough to discover those details.

For AV departments, the parallel is visible in production workflows that cross intake, scheduling, asset preparation, recording, editing, review, captioning, metadata, publishing, archiving, and support. The Uber story does not establish that every workflow should become agentic. It shows one way a large organization is finding and testing opportunities from inside the work rather than from process diagrams alone.

Runway Dev Turns Media Models Into an Enterprise Platform

Runway launched Runway Dev on July 8, describing it as a media platform for developers and enterprise teams.

The central idea is a single API that provides first-party access to Runway models alongside third-party media models. At launch, the catalog includes:

  • Runway Gen-4.5
  • Runway Aleph 2.0
  • Runway Act-Two
  • ByteDance Seedance
  • OpenAI GPT Image 2
  • ElevenLabs models

Runway says developers can change models by editing one line of code and track spending across models in one billing dashboard. The company is also introducing reusable Recipes for multi-step media workflows and says an intelligent routing layer is planned.

The enterprise controls are as important as the model list. Runway says the platform includes:

  • Contractual commitments not to train on customer data
  • Support for zero-data retention
  • Per-model cost visibility and team spending controls
  • SOC 2 Type II compliance
  • IP indemnification
  • Built-in content moderation
  • A 99.9 percent uptime commitment

These are vendor claims, but they show what generative-media infrastructure is now competing on. Image and video quality remain central, yet professional adoption also depends on data handling, retention, rights, reliability, cost visibility, and the ability to change models without rebuilding the whole application.

Runway Dev also reflects a broader consolidation pattern. The platform is no longer presenting one model as the answer to every media task. It is presenting a controlled layer where different models can be selected for generation, editing, character performance, audio, or image work.

Meta Launches Muse Image and Previews a Native-Audio Video Model

Meta Superintelligence Labs released Muse Image and previewed Muse Video on July 7. They are the lab’s first media-generation models.

Muse Image is available now. Meta says it can:

  • Follow detailed instructions
  • Edit images precisely
  • Compose from multiple references
  • Use tools such as web search and code execution
  • Review and refine its own output before returning a result

That last group of capabilities makes Muse Image different from a simple prompt-to-image system. Meta is framing it as an agentic media model that can gather context, use tools when precision matters, and spend additional inference work refining a result.

Meta also introduced Content Seal, an invisible provenance signal designed to remain detectable after an image is cropped, compressed, resized, or captured in a screenshot. A detector is in preview, and Meta says it plans to extend the system to video.

Muse Video is not generally available yet. Meta describes it as a native-audio video model with competitive prompt adherence, visual fidelity, and temporal consistency. The company also identified two areas that still need work:

  • Audio-video synchronization
  • Physically accurate fast motion

That disclosure is useful. Native audio is becoming a standard launch claim across leading video systems, but generating sound and image together does not guarantee that dialogue, movement, ambience, and physical events remain correctly synchronized.

For now, Muse Video is a preview rather than a production release. The more immediate story is Meta’s move toward media models that combine generation, editing, reference handling, tool use, and provenance.

GPT-5.6 Moves From Limited Preview to General Availability

OpenAI made the GPT-5.6 model family generally available on July 9, following the limited preview covered in the June 26 edition.

The three tiers remain:

  • Sol — the flagship model
  • Terra — the balanced model
  • Luna — the lower-cost model

The important change is access. GPT-5.6 is now rolling out through ChatGPT, Codex, and the OpenAI API rather than remaining restricted to a small preview group.

OpenAI also expanded the product story around the models:

  • Ultra mode coordinates multiple agents across parallel workstreams.
  • Program Calling lets GPT-5.6 write and run programs that coordinate tools and intermediate results.
  • A multi-agent capability is entering beta in the API.
  • GPT-5.6 adds stronger computer-use and design evaluations alongside its coding and knowledge-work results.

OpenAI also introduced ChatGPT Work, an agent designed to act across connected applications and files, break a larger goal into smaller steps, and remain with a project for hours. The company says it can gather information and produce finished materials such as documents, spreadsheets, slides, and web applications.

This release makes the late-June preview story more practical. The question is no longer only what GPT-5.6 can do in selected evaluations. It is how the model performs when broadly available inside everyday tools and longer-running workflows.

As with any agent working across organizational systems, the operational questions extend beyond benchmark scores: which applications it can access, what permissions it receives, how actions are reviewed, and what records remain after the task is complete.

LucidLink Connect Brings Frame.io Footage and Production Storage Into One Filespace

LucidLink announced a new Connect for Frame.io workflow on July 7.

Frame.io handles Camera-to-Cloud footage, dailies, and review, but supporting production assets often live elsewhere: object storage, shared drives, archive systems, audio libraries, graphics folders, or departmental cloud storage.

LucidLink Connect presents Frame.io footage and those supporting assets inside one real-time filespace without moving, migrating, or duplicating the underlying files. The original storage paths and permissions remain in place.

LucidLink says that once footage reaches Frame.io through Camera-to-Cloud, it can appear alongside external assets and become available for editing in:

  • Adobe Premiere
  • DaVinci Resolve
  • Avid Media Composer

The system streams the portions of a file needed for playback, scrubbing, or editing rather than downloading the complete media object first. The Frame.io workflow is currently available for enterprise accounts.

This is not an AI model announcement, but it matters to the AI production conversation. Agents and media models can only work effectively when they can locate and access the right assets. The data layer beneath the model—storage, permissions, paths, versions, and transfer time—continues to shape what automation is practical.

Uber’s CTO summarized the central lesson from the Agentic Pods:

“The workflow becomes the unit of automation — not the individual task.”

That distinction explains why the pods begin with observation rather than a list of automation requests. The object being redesigned is the complete flow of work, including handoffs and exceptions.

Common Threads

The Workflow Is Replacing the Task as the Unit of AI Adoption

Uber’s Agentic Pods are the clearest example, but the same pattern appears elsewhere. ChatGPT Work stays with a project across applications. Runway Dev connects multiple models inside reusable media workflows. LucidLink connects assets that previously lived in separate storage systems.

The model is becoming one component inside a longer chain of work.

Model Choice Is Moving Behind a Platform Layer

Runway Dev combines first- and third-party media models. GPT-5.6 offers different capability and cost tiers. Meta connects its reasoning and media systems. The emerging platform model assumes that no single generator will remain best for every task.

This shifts attention toward routing, permissions, cost tracking, retention, and repeatability.

Data Access Is Becoming as Important as Model Intelligence

Uber’s agents need access to the systems where departmental work happens. ChatGPT Work operates across connected applications and files. LucidLink unifies media access without moving the source files. AVTensor focuses on aligned audio and video at the training-data layer.

Across all four examples, the model cannot complete the workflow unless the data is available in a usable, correctly permissioned form.

Novel Ideas Worth Watching

Embedded Technical Builders

Uber’s model temporarily embeds an AI-proficient engineer inside a department rather than asking the department to describe its needs from a distance. The approach treats observation and domain learning as part of the engineering work.

Two-Week Workflow Discovery

The Agentic Pod schedule is unusually specific: two days of observation, one day of prioritization, two days of building, four days of broader validation, and one day to ship. Whether that pace generalizes is uncertain, but the structure is more concrete than a broad mandate to “find AI opportunities.”

Media Provenance That Survives Transformation

Meta says Content Seal remains detectable after cropping, compression, resizing, or screenshots. If reliable, provenance that survives ordinary media handling would be more useful than metadata that disappears during export.

Aligned Audio and Video at the Data Layer

Runway built AVTensor after an existing decoder silently misaligned audio and video in training data. It is a reminder that synchronization problems can begin before a model is trained, not only in the generated output.

Tip of the Week

Back to Basics, Week 7: Give It Examples Before You Ask for Output

Last week, the Back to Basics series covered Use Checklists as Guardrails: define how the result will be judged before asking AI to create or revise it.

This week’s habit is: show examples before asking for a new output.

AI often learns the intended structure faster from two representative examples than from a long paragraph describing the format.

Use this pattern:

Here are two examples of the output format I want.

Example 1:
[Paste a strong example]

Example 2:
[Paste another strong example]

Create a new version using the same:
- Structure
- Level of detail
- Tone
- Labeling pattern
- Approximate length

Do not copy the subject matter or wording from the examples.
Use them only as format references.

For AV work, examples can establish:

  • How edit notes should be formatted
  • How timecodes and speaker names should appear
  • The structure of equipment summaries
  • The level of detail in production documentation
  • The tone of client-facing updates
  • The layout of titles, descriptions, or show notes
  • The expected form of a QA report

The examples should be representative and safe to share. Remove confidential names, links, credentials, or project details before placing internal material into an AI system.

The small habit: when a format matters, provide two examples and clearly tell the model what to imitate—and what not to copy.
