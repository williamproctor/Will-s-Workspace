### Perplexity banned Time's agent ads and called them deceptive

Two weeks after Time started inserting brand-written, FAQ-formatted ad content into the markdown versions of its pages (the versions AI agents read), Perplexity confirmed on August 11 that it blocked those units from influencing its index. CCO Jesse Dwyer said the company protects users from deceptive practices "sponsored or not," and warned that publishers running markdown ads risk a trust-score downgrade in Perplexity's index. Time had labeled the units as sponsored; the objection is to the practice, not the disclosure, because the label doesn't survive retrieval. Robert Webster of TAU put the mechanism plainly: "a promotional claim can end up cited as a neutral fact."

The incentive readings are the useful part. Steven Liss noted the ban gives publishers one more reason to block Perplexity's crawlers entirely; Shiv Gupta expects other model operators to follow, partly to keep control of how their own platforms get monetized. An answer engine that blocks publisher-inserted ads is also protecting inventory it hasn't sold yet. Webster's larger frame: the internet is splitting into a human track and an agent track, and platforms are ruling on the new inventory before standards exist, which is how most ad formats have historically been settled.

**Comment window:** open now. The lazy take is "AI ads don't work." The better one is the cloaking precedent: agent-legible marketing is coming either way, and the line between "structured content agents can read" and "deceptive insertion" just started being drawn by the platforms, unilaterally.

### The New York Times told everyone what content costs: ~$4,000 a unit

On Bloomberg's Odd Lots (August 10), NYT CEO Meredith Kopit Levien disclosed that the company spent close to $2 billion last year producing about half a million works of journalism. Production costs almost never go public; that's a per-unit reference point of roughly $4,000 that every publisher and every AI lab across the table can now argue about. Her licensing framework separates three things negotiations usually blur: continuity (the deal can't cannibalize the business underneath), permission with control attached (governance of downstream use), and only then price.

The supply argument got its sharpest number the same week: Stack Overflow logged 1,442 questions in July, down 99% from its 2014 peak: the corpus that trained the models is being consumed by the products built on it. The counterweight: a bipartisan amicus brief says three firms hold 88% of AI model API revenue, so scarce supply still faces concentrated buyers. The Times sues OpenAI, Microsoft, and Perplexity while licensing to Amazon; both tracks are the same negotiation.

**Comment window:** durable. For a marketing audience the transferable idea is content as a priced, licensable asset class, plus "permission with control" as the framework brands should steal for their own content programs.

Per Google ads liaison Ginny Marvin's interview last week, it reaches campaigns meeting three conditions at once (carrying a Target CPA or ROAS, limited by budget, and delivering better than target), and the correction runs one way: over-performers drift toward their stated target, under-performers get nothing. Then September 1: campaigns using Automatically Created Assets or campaign-level broad match convert to AI Max, and the exits closed last week (Google stopped accepting new campaign-level broad match configurations on August 13; legacy creation ended August 3). The ACA cohort inherits automated query expansion unless it's switched off at ad-group level. And on August 14, Marvin announced the campaign-level language setting disappears in September — from Search and, in a scope change from the 2025 documentation, from Performance Max too, with new prioritization logic that reassigns which language's ad wins in bilingual setups.

### Lunio: AI Max campaigns carry 72% more invalid traffic, and the lines are diverging

Invalid-traffic vendor Lunio published a retail study on August 12 covering 414 million clicks from October 2025 through June: Google search campaigns with AI Max enabled were exposed to 72% more invalid traffic than search campaigns without it. The detail that makes this hard to wave away is internal to the same accounts: AI Max campaigns climbed from 2.46% invalid in Q4 2025 to 5.28% in Q2 2026 while standard search campaigns eased from 3.72% to 3.07%. Two lines diverging is a different class of finding from one line rising. AI Max also accounted for 68% of all invalid clicks in the Google dataset, and Google Shopping hit 7.51% in Q2 (roughly one in thirteen clicks). Lunio's worked example: a retailer spending $10 million a year at $3.70 CPC loses about $500,000 to wasted spend.

Context that belongs next to it: Google's own AI Max uplift claim has moved three times (14% at beta, 7% at general availability with retail excluded, 27% for exact-and-phrase-heavy adopters), independent tests found roughly 35% lower ROAS last November, and the reporting still can't split expanded from literal traffic inside a keyword row, and reports nothing on validity. Nineteen days before the mandatory migration, the measurement argument is doing the work the product documentation doesn't.

### Wall Street repriced ad tech: -22% for growing 3%, -20% for growing 53%

The Trade Desk reported $715.1 million in Q2 revenue, up 3%, guided Q3 to a year-on-year contraction, and lost roughly 22% of its market value; CEO Jeff Green called the growth "below our expectations and below the standard we hold ourselves to." AppLovin grew 53% to $1.92 billion and still fell 19.7%, because the quarter carried no new model gains for investors to extrapolate. Criteo, Taboola, and Teads all fell 24–28%; PubMatic, Magnite, and Zeta rose. The distribution doesn't sort by growth rate; the market is pricing durable position, not last quarter's number.

The number underneath the pattern comes from Madison & Wall: Amazon, Google, and Meta took roughly 56% of US advertising last year, heading for about 58% this year, while the open internet is projected to shrink 1.4% inside a growing market. Meanwhile the measurement layer is leaving public view entirely: IAS went private, LiveRamp's Publicis buyout votes today, Innovid's gone, Nielsen is absorbing DoubleVerify, and Comscore disclosed an 85% collapse in adjusted EBITDA alongside a restructuring and a 20% CEO pay cut. One Comscore bet lands directly in the AEO lane: it's pitching measurement built on observed panelist prompts rather than the synthetic prompts most AI-visibility tools run on. The category is consolidating before it's standardized.

### The AEO measurement stack shipped counts without connections

Google Search Console's generative AI performance report went live for everyone around August 11: impressions in AI features, by page, country, device, and date. It carries no click data and no query data. You can count machine reads of your content, but you can't see what people asked or whether anyone arrived. The same week, OpenAI moved ChatGPT's Sources button into a three-dot menu (attribution demoted, not removed), and Microsoft Clarity shipped a card ranking which AI operators scrape most and refer least, with a sample showing a 6,000-to-1 crawl-to-referral ratio.

The buy side has the mirror problem: Digiday reported CMOs can't link AI visibility to sales despite spending on tools that rate brand presence in model answers. Publisher and advertiser now sit in symmetrical positions: one knows roughly how often machines read its work, the other how often machines mention its brand, and neither number connects to a payment. The tooling race to supply the missing connection is on: Clarity's ratio card, last week's branded/non-branded citation split, Jellyfish adjusting ad spend by share-of-model, Comscore's observed-prompt pitch.

### Congress moved on stealth crawlers as one publisher priced the problem: 25% of hosting costs

Three House members introduced the Stealth Bot Prohibition Act (authored by the News/Media Alliance; a New York version already passed): AI crawlers would have to disclose identity and purpose to site hosts, at $53,000 per violation. The commercial math arrived with it: Axel Springer's Amelia Binder said 25% of Politico's hosting costs now go to bot management, and the Tampa Bay Times described bot loads degrading service for human readers. Disclosure sounds procedural; it's actually the precondition for any pricing mechanism, because an anonymous crawler can't be charged, rate-limited by contract, or offered a premium tier.

The substrate data published the same week explains the urgency: a Stanford study found 5% of IP addresses send 55% of all web requests, and 44% of client IPs carry two or more user agents. The signals frequency caps and household matching are built on are thinning at the same time the machine traffic they'd need to police is growing.
