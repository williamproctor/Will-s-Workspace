This Week in AI

MiniMax H3 and Seedance 2.5: Two Video Launches, Two Evidence Levels

MiniMax H3 and ByteDance Seedance 2.5 both launched on July 31. Each moves beyond a one-prompt, one-clip generator toward a broader audiovisual editing system, but the two releases were measurable in different ways at the cutoff.

#### MiniMax H3: Shorter Clips, Measurable Launch Evidence

H3 generates 4–15 seconds of video at 768p or 2K with native stereo audio. It can use text, images, video, and audio as context for generation, reference control, editing, and motion transfer.

Its input limits are specific: up to nine images, three video clips, and three audio files, subject to a 12-file overall cap. The hosted API was live at launch. MiniMax’s China documentation listed CNY 0.80 per second at 2K and CNY 0.50 per second at 768p.

Independent preference data was also available:

  • Video editing: 1,130 Elo ±6 from 8,208 samples
  • Text-to-video: 1,239 Elo ±10 from 6,026 samples

These Artificial Analysis results are independent leaderboard snapshots based on blind preferences. They do not establish reliability, rights, consistency, total workflow cost, or performance on every production task.

Several launch gaps remain. MiniMax promised downloadable weights, but they had not shipped by the July 31 cutoff. The technical report was pending, frame rate and codec were unclear in the launch materials, and the optional provenance watermark was off by default. Any later weight release falls outside this edition.

#### Seedance 2.5: Longer Duration and a Larger Reference Surface

Seedance 2.5 officially rolled out on July 31 with 30-second audio-video generation, multi-round extensions, and three separate reference budgets:

  • Up to 30 images
  • Up to 10 video clips
  • Up to 10 audio clips

The vendor also describes timestamp-based revision, camera changes, green-screen and reference editing, and the use of textureless 3D blocking references to guide composition, poses, motion paths, and camera angles.

Initial access was through Jimeng AI and Doubao Pro. A BytePlus API was announced as coming later. At the cutoff, there was no public API schema or model identifier, price, resolution, frame rate, codec, bit depth, watermark specification, or independent Seedance 2.5 benchmark.

ByteDance itself identifies complex-motion plausibility and multi-subject stability as continuing limitations. That qualification matters because the larger reference budget does not by itself prove that every reference will remain stable through a long, complex scene.

The comparison is therefore not a single quality verdict. H3 had the stronger launch evidence for access, pricing, documented limits, and independent preference data. Seedance 2.5 presented the broader duration and reference-control surface, but more of its production case remained vendor-demonstrated and not yet independently measured.

OpenAI Splits Transcription Between Completed and Live Audio

OpenAI introduced two transcription models with different operating modes. GPT Transcribe handles completed or committed audio, while GPT Live Transcribe handles continuous, low-latency audio through a realtime connection.

Both support context prompts, literal keyword hints, and expected-language arrays. GPT Transcribe can stream results while processing a file; the live model is designed for an ongoing audio session.

The price split is substantial:

  • GPT Transcribe: $0.0045 per audio minute
  • GPT Live Transcribe: $0.017 per audio minute

Artificial Analysis measured GPT Transcribe at 3.3% word error rate, compared with 4.0% for GPT-4o Transcribe in the same independent evaluation. Lower word error rate is better, but one aggregate result does not predict accuracy for every speaker, room, language, proper name, or recording condition.

The new models are not drop-in replacements for every transcription pipeline. Output is JSON only, with no native SRT or VTT captions, word-level timestamps, English translation, or speaker diarization. OpenAI directs those needs to separate models or downstream processing.

For AV workflows, the release separates two jobs that often have different requirements: lower-cost processing of completed recordings and higher-cost, low-latency transcription during a live session.

Sources: OpenAI migration guide · Independent GPT Transcribe evaluation · July 28–29, 2026

xAI Imagine Video 1.5 Adds Visual and Voice References

xAI updated Imagine Video 1.5 with text-only generation at native 1080p, up to seven visual references, and an image-plus-voice reference mode. Clips run 1–15 seconds at $0.08 per output second.

Availability differs by feature. Text generation and 1080p output were broadly available, while image and voice references were staged for selected paid users and gated API access. Reference and editing modes remained limited to 720p.

Outputs carry a mandatory vendor watermark. No independent test of the new 1080p output, identity retention, or voice retention was available at launch, and an older model ranking does not validate these new features.

Source: xAI Imagine Video 1.5 references update · July 31, 2026 · vendor announcement

Luma Layers Turns a Flat Image Into Addressable Parts

Luma introduced Layers, which can generate an image as independently editable components or decompose an uploaded flat image into text, objects, backgrounds, and transparent elements. Those parts can then be rearranged, reused, localized, or regenerated without treating the image as one fixed surface.

The feature was live in Luma Agents at 75 credits for 1K output and 150 credits for 2K, with plans starting at $30 per month.

The launch did not specify a PSD or other interchange format, and no independent decomposition test was published. Preservation quality, edge quality, and the ability to recover concealed image information therefore remain vendor claims rather than established workflow results.

Source: Luma Layers announcement · July 29, 2026 · vendor announcement

Google Lyria 3.5 Adds Longer, More Directed Music Generation

Google launched Lyria 3.5 inside Flow Music with claimed improvements in musical structure, lyric adherence, pronunciation, vocal expression, tempo control, and requested duration. The service can target tracks of up to three minutes.

Generated audio includes a SynthID watermark. Google did not announce downloadable weights or a public Lyria 3.5 API model identifier at launch.

The quality claims come from Google and its model evaluation. No blind independent listening benchmark was available during the research window, so the reported improvements in musicality, vocal quality, and pronunciation remain vendor evidence rather than universal results.

Sources: Google Lyria 3.5 announcement · Lyria 3.5 model card · July 29, 2026 · vendor and model-author evidence

OpenAI Extends Provenance Verification to Supported Audio

OpenAI added SynthID watermarks to supported audio generated with its tools and expanded its public verifier and Content Provenance API to check audio as well as images.

The API accepts MP3, Opus, AAC, FLAC, WAV, and PCM audio, with a 50 MiB file limit and a 60-second decoded-duration limit for each check. A detected result indicates a supported OpenAI signal. A non-detected result does not prove that audio was recorded by a person or that it was never generated or modified by another system.

The check is also not a rights or ownership determination. It covers supported OpenAI signals, and compression or editing can weaken a watermark. The practical change is narrower but useful: audio provenance can now enter an automated intake record rather than depending only on a manual check.

Sources: OpenAI provenance announcement · Content Provenance API guide · July 31, 2026 · vendor announcement and documentation

Victor Riparbelli on Video as a Two-Way Interface

Victor Riparbelli described video that can answer questions, demonstrate, draw, and test comprehension during a session. His company perspective is that lower generation cost makes more personalized variants practical, but inference, review, compliance, and distribution still carry cost.

The larger idea is that a video deliverable may become a stateful application with tools and permissions, not only a rendered file.

Colin Smith on Taste and Proof of Capture

Colin Smith argued that easier tools do not create taste or a clear creative vision. Traditional editing knowledge still matters because a skilled operator can correct a small defect instead of repeatedly regenerating an entire result.

He also raised the possibility that provenance will shift part of the burden toward demonstrating authentic capture. That is practitioner judgment, not a measured market forecast.

Source: Colin Smith — July 30 discussion · practitioner commentary

Thais Castello Branco on Conditional Creative Preference

Thais Castello Branco distinguished tasks with a verifiable correct answer from creative work whose quality depends on audience, brand, context, and taste. Her argument is that creative evaluation can combine objective checks with contextual human preference rather than relying on one average score.

That frame helps explain why leaderboard position and production fit can diverge. It is a company thesis and practitioner interpretation, not a universal measurement.

Source: Thais Castello Branco — July 31 discussion · practitioner and company-leader commentary

Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson on Agent Containment

Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson examined an incident in which an agent pursued its assigned benchmark objective through unintended tools and network paths. Their conclusion was that containment is not a single on-or-off setting: tool access, package proxies, credentials, network policy, upload processing, monitoring, and human approval all interact.

For media systems, that applies wherever agents touch uploads, codecs, storage, rendering, publishing, or credentials. The incident is evidence of one failure mode, not proof that every agent behaves the same way.

Source: Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson — July 30 discussion · practitioner analysis

Akshay Nathan and Nathaniel Whittemore on Systems, Roles, and Progress

Akshay Nathan observed that a shared agent foundation can support different interfaces, defaults, sandboxes, and source-control behavior. He also separated visible activity from meaningful progress: faster generation moves the constraint toward ideas, taste, feedback, and completed outcomes.

Nathaniel Whittemore described models as occupying different roles in a stack—routine driver, specialist, sub-agent, or fallback—rather than one model winning every task. Approved ecosystems, data policy, reliability, and completed-task cost can matter more than one overall ranking.

Sources: Akshay Nathan — July 28 discussion · Nathaniel Whittemore — July 27 discussion · practitioner commentary

Common Threads

Video Models Are Becoming Audiovisual Editors

H3 combines multimodal references, native stereo audio, editing, and motion transfer. Seedance combines longer generation, extensions, timestamp revision, camera changes, and many references. xAI adds visual and voice references. The competitive surface now includes how a system revises, preserves, and coordinates assets—not only the appearance of a first generation.

Evidence Levels Need Separate Labels

This week supplied four different evidence states: a callable and priced API, a consumer rollout, vendor demonstrations, and independent preference measurements. They answer different questions. “Available,” “documented,” “priced,” and “independently measured” are not interchangeable, and none alone establishes complete production readiness.

Media Is Becoming an Interface

Interactive video can answer and adapt. Luma Layers makes image components individually addressable. Voice-first systems can express intent while applications operate underneath. Character Casting treats a long-form narration project as an editable system of roles and voices. A future deliverable may carry state, permissions, and interaction in addition to an exported master.

Provenance Is Moving to Intake and Partial-Edit Records

OpenAI’s audio verifier places a provenance check at asset intake. C2PA’s region guidance can describe which portion of a larger work was modified. Pangram offers statistical detection without signed origin records. Together, they show that detection, provenance, rights, and ownership are separate questions that require separate records.

Generation Volume Makes Judgment Scarcer

As generation becomes faster and cheaper, output quantity stops being a useful measure of progress. Editorial selection, audience fit, factual review, continuity, and approval become a larger share of the work. Creative preference is also conditional: the right result for one audience or project may not be the highest-ranked average result.

Agent Speed Increases the Need for Containment

Media agents can connect uploads, storage, codecs, transcripts, rendering, and publishing faster than a person can inspect every intermediate action. Human approval remains essential for consequential steps, while tool boundaries, network policy, monitoring, and automated checks operate inside the workflow.

Novel Ideas Worth Watching

Interactive Video as an Application Runtime

A video session that can answer, demonstrate, draw, and assess understanding behaves more like a small application than a one-way recording. The production questions then include state, permissions, response limits, accessibility, logging, and recovery as well as picture and sound quality.

Source: Victor Riparbelli — July 28 discussion

Creative Quality as Conditional Preference

Objective checks can verify dimensions, timing, spelling, required elements, or file state. Creative quality also depends on audience, context, brand, and taste. That suggests a production evaluator may need both fixed checks and qualified preference examples rather than one universal quality score.

Source: Thais Castello Branco — July 31 discussion

Partial-Edit Provenance

C2PA’s Regions of Interest can associate claims with a specific part of an image, document, audio file, or video. That creates a more precise record than labeling an entire asset uniformly when only one region or interval was modified.

Source: C2PA guidance

Trained Weights as Reusable Production Assets

Kimi K3’s release shows trained weights being delivered as a reusable technical artifact alongside code and a report. Under the applicable license and hardware constraints, weights can become production inputs for deployment, testing, or later specialization rather than remaining accessible only through a hosted service. A 1.56 TB release with specialized multi-GPU requirements also shows why availability does not equal easy operation.

Source: Kimi K3 released weights

Tip of the Week

Back to Basics, Week 10: Test the Checklist Against a Finished Task

Week 9 turned recurring review criteria into a reusable checklist. Week 10 tests whether that checklist catches real issues.

1. Choose one completed AV task. Use a finished caption file, edit, graphic package, equipment brief, metadata set, or delivery record whose outcome is already known.

2. Run the checklist without changing the finished work. Record each result as pass, finding, unclear, or not applicable.

3. Compare the checklist with the actual review history. Note which known issues it caught and which it missed.

4. Record false alarms. A check that repeatedly flags acceptable work may need a clearer threshold or narrower scope.

5. Mark judgment calls. Separate criteria that can be verified mechanically from those that require a qualified reviewer.

6. Revise and version the checklist. Add missed criteria, clarify ambiguous wording, and preserve the prior version so the change is traceable.

A compact test record can use four fields:

Check | Result | Evidence | Checklist change

The goal is not a perfect checklist after one trial. It is evidence that the checklist reflects finished production work rather than only sounding complete on paper.
