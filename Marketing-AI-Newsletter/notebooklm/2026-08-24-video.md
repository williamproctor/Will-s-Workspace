### A million people clicked LinkedIn's slop button. Flagged posts lost 40% of their views.

LinkedIn CPO Hari Srinivasan posted Thursday that the "seems like AI slop" button, live since July 30, has been clicked more than a million times, and that members now see 40% fewer views on content LinkedIn classifies as slop. Behind the button, The Register notes, LinkedIn's classifiers were already catching hundreds of thousands of slop comments a day, and the platform removed its own AI "enhance your post" feature in July.

The comment section under Srinivasan's post carries the obvious objection: LinkedIn spent two years nudging members to write with AI and now hands readers a button to punish the results. Both things are true, which is what makes this a policy change rather than a feature.

### Three platforms narrowed manual price controls in the same week

Google's bidding recalibration went live Monday, August 17: budget-capped campaigns on Target CPA or ROAS now optimize toward the stated target instead of the lower cost the budget cap used to produce, a one-way correction across five campaign types that raises spend for accounts that had been beating their targets. Friday, Microsoft confirmed that Max CPC manual bidding disappears for new non-portfolio campaigns on October 1 (existing campaigns keep it for now). And ChatGPT Ads made "Maximize results" the preselected strategy for eligible new ad groups, with a caveat sitting in OpenAI's own documentation: the strategy "prioritizes result volume and does not guarantee delivery against a specific CPA, CPC, ROAS, or other cost-efficiency target at this time." The only ceiling is a per-ad-group switch to manual max bids; there's no account-level default.

The ChatGPT stack deserves the closest read because each piece loosens a constraint: daily budgets became seven-day averages in July with no opt-out, view-through conversions arrived August 18 in reporting but sit outside bidding and CPA math entirely, and Automatic Advanced Matching became the default August 17. Individually defensible; stacked, they describe an account where the spend limit is elastic, the bid has no ceiling by default, and part of the measured outcome can't be optimized against. ### ChatGPT ads reach 31 European markets today, stripped of personalization

OpenAI announced August 18 that ads go live across the 27 EU states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, and Switzerland on Monday, August 24 (this morning, if you're reading on time). Free and Go plans see ads; Plus, Pro, and Enterprise don't. The GDPR-shaped detail: personalized ads aren't available in the EEA or Switzerland at launch. Targeting runs on the current conversation, language, coarse location, and device; earlier chats, saved memories, and ad history stay out of it. Several European outlets framed the launch as consent-gated, but there's nothing to consent to yet; consent becomes relevant only when OpenAI adds personalization as a second step.

The timing marks six months since the February US pilot, after Canada, Australia, New Zealand, the UK, Mexico, Brazil, Japan, and South Korea. Booking runs through OpenAI's sales team and partner agencies; the self-serve Ads Manager still shows all 31 markets as "coming soon," expected later this quarter. For European teams, the interesting test is whether conversation-context targeting alone performs, because that's the version of AI advertising the region's regulators are steering everyone toward.

### Apple Maps ads went live with 15% back for early movers

Apple switched on ad delivery inside Maps on Friday, August 21, a week after opening campaign creation to US and Canadian businesses. Two placements: Suggested Places, which surfaces ads before a query is typed (closer to discovery than search), and search results once intent narrows. Small businesses run automated campaigns through Apple Business; agencies get keywords, scheduling, location targeting, and an API through Apple Ads. The launch incentive is concrete: 15% of monthly spend back as a statement credit for up to a year, capped at $1,000 a month, for bookings made by credit card before October 11.

Apple's pitch numbers are Apple-reported: over a billion business searches on Maps a month, with one in two leading to an action like a call or directions. What's structurally notable is the targeting: location and time, no identity profiles, in what the sales team calls "a privacy-first environment." Between this and Europe's contextual-only ChatGPT ads, identity-free auctions stopped being a compromise this week and became a product tier.

### Walmart's ads grew 43%, and its AI shopper's users spend 40% more

Walmart's Q2 FY27 report (August 20) gave retail media its strongest print of the season: global advertising up 38%, and Walmart Connect in the US up 43% excluding Vizio. The number with longer implications came from CEO John Furner on the call: customers using Sparky, Walmart's AI shopping agent, are up 70% year over year, and they spend 40% more per order than non-users (retailer-reported, and up from a 35% gap last quarter). The mechanism he described is cross-channel purchase history: Sparky builds baskets that skip what you already bought, online or in store. CFO John David Rainey listed "leading in agentic experiences" among the company's strategic priorities.

The ledger has both columns: the Vibe acquisition (self-serve CTV) now carries a quantified ~20-basis-point operating-income headwind after June guidance said no impact, and the stock fell about 9% premarket on the wider print even with raised full-year guidance. Still, this is the first time a retailer's CEO has attached hard order-value numbers to an in-house shopping agent, which moves agentic commerce from thesis to line item.

### Uber's €825 million fine prices automation without human review

The Dutch Data Protection Authority, working with France's CNIL, fined Uber €824,990,000 on Friday for fully automated driver deactivations between 2018 and 2022: fraud suspicions triggered temporary blocks and low customer ratings triggered temporary or permanent ones, with what the regulator called a complete absence of human intervention, plus inadequate disclosure to drivers that software was deciding. It's the second-largest GDPR fine ever, calculated at the 4%-of-global-turnover maximum under Article 22, the provision governing automated individual decisions. Uber is appealing: "We strongly disagree with this decision and disproportionate fine."

The marketing translation is direct. Article 22 covers decisions that significantly affect people made without human review, and platform ecosystems run on exactly those: ad-account suspensions, policy enforcement, monetization cutoffs. This fine landed the same week three ad platforms narrowed manual controls, and that's the asymmetry to sit with: platforms are automating the decisions while regulators start pricing unaccountable automation. The complaint began with more than 170 French drivers and a human-rights association in 2020; six years later it's a billion-dollar precedent.

### Two studies priced AI trust: a 31.5% label tax and a 6% delegation rate

The IAB published version 2 of its AI Transparency & Disclosure Framework on August 18, and the load-bearing number inside is from NYU Stern: telling consumers an ad was made with generative AI cut its click-through rate by 31.5%. The framework uses that cost to argue for materiality-based labeling rather than blanket disclosure: synthetic spokespeople, digital twins, and fabricated scenarios get labels; color correction and stylized imagery don't. Implementation is specified down to the glyph (the ✨ character or "AI-generated" text at WCAG-AA contrast, C2PA metadata underneath) on a 24-month roadmap. It's voluntary, and it arrives after the law: EU AI Act Article 50 has applied since August 2, with California and New York running their own regimes.

StackAdapt's "AI Delegation Gap" report (August 19, fielded by NewtonX among 500 marketing professionals) supplied the other half of the trust ledger: 91% of organizations use AI tools, 88% report performance gains, and 6% say they almost always act on in-platform AI recommendations. Comfort falls from 90% when AI recommends to 50% when it acts autonomously, even with proven performance; 42% ignore recommendations as generic; 17% report no clear accountability for bad AI decisions. Set those numbers against this week's default-automation moves and the shape of 2026 is visible: the platforms' confidence in delegation is running about eight times ahead of their customers'.

### Identity-free launched as a tier, not a fallback

Apple Maps ads target location and time with no identity profiles, sold as "privacy-first." ChatGPT's European ads run on conversation context only, because GDPR made personalization the second step. Two major auctions launched identity-free in the same week and neither apologized for it, which is worth remembering the next time a vendor equates targeting depth with performance.

Walmart's agent users spend 40% more per order while 6% of marketers act on their ad platform's AI recommendations. Consumers are handing decisions to agents faster than professionals are, possibly because Sparky explains itself with a basket you can inspect, and a bid recommendation doesn't.
