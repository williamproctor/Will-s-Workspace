# Signal & Scale — Week of July 27, 2026

> **The AI ad wars went main-character this week.** Meta answered the "doomers" with an optimism spot scored, apparently unironically, to a David Bowie song about human extinction, while the wreckage of Anthropic's "hard questions" ad was still smoldering on X. Same week: X deleted 42,000 AI reply bots, and Alphabet told investors that ads inside AI search are now real revenue, even as new data showed buying a placement doesn't buy a citation. The pattern underneath it all: authenticity and earned visibility keep getting more valuable at exactly the moment automation gets cheaper.

## The Conversation

### Meta answers the AI doomers with butterflies and a Bowie song about extinction

Meta launched "The Future Is for Everyone" on July 23: a one-minute AI-optimism spot that opens on doomy black-and-white headlines and blooms into color. The voiceover: "Call us optimists. Call us dreamers. Call us whatever the hell you want. But we're betting on people, and we like those odds." Zuckerberg posted it personally. Meta CMO Denise Moreno carried the LinkedIn version ("we think AI will empower people to do more of what they love").

The dunks wrote themselves. The soundtrack is David Bowie's "Five Years," a song about Earth learning it has five years left, with the apocalyptic verses trimmed out. TechCrunch ran it as an AI optimism ad "set to a song about human extinction." Engadget noted the campaign is "conspicuously light on AI," and it is: no product actually does anything in the spot. The timing is legible to everyone, too. Meta reports earnings Wednesday with hundreds of billions in AI capex to justify.

The richer subplot is the contrast with Anthropic's "There's Hope in Hard Questions" (July 9, roughly 3.3M views on X), which took the opposite bet. Cemeteries, burning houses, existential questions — and it got mocked for it, most famously by Sam Altman: "i thought this was satire." One detail worth stealing for client decks: the same Anthropic ad was praised by creative directors on LinkedIn and shredded on X and YouTube. Same creative, opposite receptions, different networks.

**Comment window:** wide open through Meta's earnings Wednesday. The cheap take is dunking on the song choice. The differentiated take is what these two campaigns reveal about audience-message fit and how AI companies should market AI at all.

### X deletes 42,000 AI reply bots, and LinkedIn is quietly doing the same to AI comments

On July 24, X head of product Nikita Bier announced: "We found 42,000 accounts automating replies using chatbots and have removed them from the platform. X's core value is providing an authentic pulse on humanity — and using AI to programmatically engage with users without a human in the loop runs counter to our mission." No warnings, no grace period. It's the sharpest strike yet in a crackdown that has removed more than 1.7 million bots since early 2026, and unlike earlier spam sweeps, this one hit tools that real startups and growth teams use to automate engagement.

LinkedIn is reportedly moving the same direction: its 2026 algorithm changes suppress generic AI comments out of "Most Relevant" and restrict repeat offenders (secondary reporting via Startup Fortune, so treat as reported rather than confirmed).

The read for growth teams: AI-automated engagement is being reclassified from tactic to violation on the two platforms that matter most for B2B thought leadership. Draft-with-human-review workflows survive. Autonomous engagement products just lost their addressable market.

**Comment window:** open now. Every founder who bought an "AI engagement" tool is quietly re-reading the contract, and a grounded post on what still works will land all week.

### Google's AI told customers a bakery was closing. It wasn't.

Anna Mae's Bakery owner Amanda Herrfort found out from a customer's screenshot that Google's AI Overview was telling people her shop permanently closes July 31. The AI invented the date. Google blamed thin web data. The story went viral because it's the perfect miniature of the AI-visibility problem: the machine now speaks for your brand whether or not you're publishing, and silence is exactly what it hallucinates into.

## This Week in Marketing AI

### Alphabet Q2: ads inside AI search are real revenue now, and buying one doesn't buy a citation

Alphabet reported Q2 on July 22. Search advertising grew 17% to $63.3 billion while the open-web Network line fell 1% to $7.3 billion. The part that matters for marketers is where the growth is coming from. AI Mode has passed 1 billion monthly users, and Google is monetizing it directly: contextual sitelinks, Direct Offers (the Chewy, Gap, and L'Oréal pilot, with IHG next), and Highlighted Answers, which chief business officer Philipp Schindler described as "clearly marked sponsored links inside list responses." Those formats debuted at Google Marketing Live in May and showed up on an earnings call as revenue drivers nine weeks later.

AI Max is out of beta with more than 500,000 advertisers. Google claims a 15% average lift in conversions or value at similar ROAS. That's a Google-measured number, and independent tests published last November found the opposite on cost per conversion, a discrepancy nobody has resolved publicly. Dynamic Search Ads retire in September with automatic upgrades into AI Max, which makes the AI format the default rather than the option. Sundar Pichai repeated that Google sends "billions of clicks to websites every week" through AI features. Hold that against Ahrefs' finding that AI Overviews correlate with a 58% click-through drop for top-ranked pages. Both can be true. Denominators matter.

Then the kicker: SE Ranking found AI Mode showed a text ad on 29% of tested commercial keywords, but the advertiser's domain appeared among the answer's cited sources only 11% of the time. Paid placement, citations, and rankings are behaving like three separate surfaces. Buying one does not buy the others.

### August 2: AI-content labels become enforceable in the EU, with fines up to 3% of turnover

The European Commission published its Article 50 guidelines and finalized the Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content on July 20. The EU AI Act's transparency obligations become applicable **August 2**, with fines up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher. Google announced July 24 that it's signing the Code. Its own documentation still keeps liability with the advertiser.

Two details matter most for marketing teams. First, the artistic carve-out does not cover advertising: Commission guidance explicitly lists a synthetic spokesperson selling a product as content needing the full label. Assistive editing (retouching, color work, cropping) stays exempt. Second, the EU is one layer of a stack. New York's synthetic-performer disclosure has been live since June 9 at $1,000 to $5,000 per violation, India's rules since February, and California's SB 942/AB 853 are synced to the same August 2 date. Google spent the month shipping the plumbing: an AI label setting across five ads products, attestation fields in the Google Ads and DV360 APIs. All of it records declarations that no system validates and that remain the advertiser's legal problem.

### ChatGPT Ads ships conversion bidding on top of an auction nobody can see into

OpenAI rolled out the toolkit performance marketers were waiting for: conversion-optimized (oCPC) campaigns, geo exclusions, AppsFlyer and Adjust integrations, hashed-data advanced matching, a bulk API, and product cards showing prices and ratings. Daily budgets move to seven-day rolling averages from July 27. The platform that started with $200,000 minimums now has none.

Demand is arriving faster than the reporting. Adthena tracks 7,378 advertisers in ChatGPT, up 97-fold in a quarter from an admittedly small base, led by Booking.com. But the native Ads Manager shows advertisers only their own numbers. No impression share, no auction insights. UK advertisers have watched costs swing 39% to 278% with no visible cause, and you're bidding on loose conversational prompt categories rather than keywords you can enumerate. This auction sits roughly where paid search sat two decades ago, except the spend is arriving at modern scale before the reporting exists.

### Similarweb: ChatGPT falls to half of gen-AI traffic, and citations point at different pages than clicks

Similarweb's 2026 Generative AI Landscape report landed this week: 9.5 billion monthly web visits to gen-AI platforms, up 70% year over year. ChatGPT's share of standalone gen-AI web visits fell from above 80% in late 2024 to just above 50% by May. June snapshots of the same panel put Gemini around 27% and Claude around 9%. For anyone doing AI-visibility work, four findings stand out:

- ChatGPT's citation rate jumped from roughly 1.3% to 6.8% of answers in a year, led by travel (22.6%) and retail (13.5%). Reviews and user-generated content supply 28.9% of top citations; news another 26.0%.
- Citations and referrals diverge: 65% of cited URLs sit two or more folders deep in a site, while 58.8% of referral traffic lands on homepages.
- AI referral traffic converts at 7.1%, second only to paid search at 7.8%.
- Ads appeared in 26% of US desktop ChatGPT chats in June, up from 14% in May, at a 0.50% click-through rate. Which means roughly three-quarters of even the most ad-saturated assistant surface is still earned exposure.

Caveats before anyone builds a deck on this: these are panel estimates, and the vendors disagree with each other. Similarweb puts AI Overviews in 43% of US searches where Adthena's pulse says 18%, and their UK ChatGPT ad numbers are flatly incompatible. There is no standard measurement yet.

### WARC: 88% more creative, 45% better. The brief is the bottleneck.

WARC's "The New Creative Advantage" (produced with TikTok and LIONS Advisory; 400 marketers across four markets; covered July 22) found 88% of marketers producing more creative volume since adopting AI. Only 45% report significantly better quality. The study locates that 43-point gap in the brief: 67% feed generative models demographic data as the primary input, while 59% of the same population says demographic segmentation no longer works. Just 17% consistently add community or audience insight. TikTok's Andy Yang, in the foreword, calls it "not a technology gap, it is an intelligence gap." (Disclosure: TikTok commissioned research that concludes marketers need the cultural signal TikTok sells. The data stands; the alignment belongs on the record.)

The mechanism is the useful part. AI compresses production, which removes the human layers that used to compensate for thin briefs. The brief now travels almost directly to the finished asset, so input quality matters more than ever, at the exact moment most teams left it unchanged.

**Source:** [PPC Land weekly analysis](https://ppc.land/marketers-brief-ai-with-the-demographic-data-they-say-no-longer-works/) · covered July 22, 2026

### Fractl: 81% of buyers still call it SEO, and buzzword pitches are the top red flag

Fractl surveyed 343 US marketing decision-makers responsible for AI visibility (released July 21). 81% still say "SEO" when discussing AI search strategy internally; 19% have adopted "GEO," 10% "AEO." When buyers search for help, "AI search optimization" (46%) and "SEO" (24%) dominate. "GEO" gets 12% of searches, "AEO" 3%. Heavy buzzword use is the single biggest vendor red flag at 36%, and case studies outrank acronym fluency roughly four to one. The budgets moved anyway: an average 24% of search and content spend now goes to AI visibility, with financial services leading at 39%. (Disclosure: Fractl sells adjacent services, and the sample is US-only at 343.)

## Common Threads

### Authenticity is becoming platform policy, not just taste

X deleted 42,000 AI reply bots for lacking "a human in the loop." LinkedIn reportedly suppresses generic AI comments. Meta ships selfie-verification badges. The platforms that sold reach are now defending the authenticity of engagement itself, which raises the value of genuinely human commentary at the same moment the supply of fake commentary gets purged.

### AI search is now a paid channel, and paid doesn't buy cited

Google booked AI Mode ad revenue and OpenAI shipped conversion bidding. Yet advertisers' domains appear among AI-cited sources just 11% of the time on ad-carrying keywords, and roughly three-quarters of US desktop ChatGPT chats carry no ads at all. Paid presence, citations, and rankings are three separate surfaces sharing a screen. Budget them separately.

### Production outran verification

Marketers make 88% more creative and can verify quality on 45% of it. Agent launches ship precise time savings and no accuracy rates. Attestation APIs record declarations nobody validates. Across the stack, generation scaled faster than the evaluation layer around it, and vendors publish the variable that's easy to measure.

### The measurement layer is unsettled, so anchor accordingly

Similarweb says AI Overviews appear in 43% of US searches. Adthena says 18%. Their UK ChatGPT ad-penetration numbers are mutually exclusive. Any strategy narrative or client deck anchored on a single vendor's panel inherits that uncertainty and should say so out loud.

### "Meta and Anthropic just ran the same ad with opposite emotions. Both missed."

The most instructive marketing case study of the year is AI companies trying to market AI, and the reception data is sitting in public. Meta's July 23 optimism spot drew extinction-song dunks and a "conspicuously light on AI" review six days before earnings. Anthropic's doom-toned ad pulled 3.3M views and Altman's satire treatment on X, yet creative directors praised it on LinkedIn. ### "X just published its algorithm's values: automated engagement is a violation now."

Forty-two thousand accounts deleted for AI replies reads like a spam story until you notice whose tools got hit. Bier's July 24 post drew the line at "a human in the loop," 1.7 million bots have been purged since early 2026, and LinkedIn is reportedly demoting generic AI comments in parallel. Every team running AI engagement tools is deciding what to do about it this week. A concrete post on what survives (draft-with-review) and what dies (autopilot) is immediately useful and lands on the right side of the purge.

Window: this week, while the purge is fresh.

### "AI search became a paid channel this week. Citations are still earned."

Google will now happily sell you a spot inside the AI answer. It will not sell you a citation. Alphabet cited AI Mode formats as revenue on July 22; SE Ranking found advertisers' domains among cited sources just 11% of the time; Similarweb shows about 74% of US desktop ChatGPT chats carrying no ads and recommended brands getting 2–4x the follow-on visits. This is the first week anyone can say "AI search monetization is real" with an earnings call behind it, and the paid-versus-earned frame for AI answers is still unclaimed.

Window: durable, but freshest before the earnings cycle moves on.

### "Would your ads survive August 2?"

In six days, an unlabeled synthetic spokesperson in the EU can cost 3% of worldwide turnover. Article 50 becomes applicable August 2, advertising is explicitly excluded from the artistic carve-out, Google signed the Code on July 24 and still points liability at the advertiser, New York has been live since June 9, and California syncs to the same date. A practical decision-tree post ("which of your assets need a label?") is genuinely useful and reads as the adult in the room.

Window: hard deadline this week.

### "Your buyers still call it SEO."

The industry renamed the discipline three times before the customer renamed it once. Fractl's July 21 study: 81% say "SEO" internally, "AEO" gets 3% of help-seeking searches, buzzword-heavy pitches are the top vendor red flag at 36%, and case studies beat acronym fluency four to one, all while 24% of search and content budgets already moved to AI visibility. For a team that uses the AEO label daily, naming that tension out loud is what makes the post credible.
