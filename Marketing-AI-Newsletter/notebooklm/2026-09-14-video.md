### A sportsbook deepfaked a fan two days after ESPN made her famous

ESPN's cameras lingered on Ole Miss alum Sara Smith during the Rebels' win over Louisville on Sunday, September 6. ### "Google's Latest SCAM!": promotional credits revoked after the money was spent

PPC consultant David Melamed posted that two client accounts had Google Ads promotional credits marked "Invalidated" more than a month after spending the $3,200 required to earn them. **Comment window:** open. The practical post writes itself: treat promotional credits as provisional until they survive two billing cycles, put that in writing to the client before the spend happens, and screenshot the terms at acceptance.

### Google shipped its "largest reduction in quality" to Europe and said so out loud

On Tuesday, September 8, Google rolled out redesigned results across 30 European markets to comply with the Digital Markets Act, after a €460 million fine in July for favoring its own shopping, hotel, transport, and sports results and a 60-day deadline backed by penalties of up to 5% of worldwide daily turnover. Reuters had the story first, with the line that defined the week from an unnamed Google official: "the largest reduction in quality of service at the world's most popular internet search engine in its 29-year search history." Nick Fox, Google's SVP for knowledge and information, said the changes "degrade the user experience for Europeans" by "boosting online intermediaries at the expense of local businesses," and that users outside the EU won't be affected.

The layout is the remedy. Search Central documented two EEA-only units the same day: an aggregator unit that gives one approved comparison service a block at the top (the top-ranked provider expanded by default, the rest behind a dropdown), and a supplier unit for direct businesses that strips out date filters, live prices, and descriptive tags. The vacation-rentals unit is gone; the changes extend into AI Overviews and AI Mode. Google's supporting claims (earlier DMA changes cut free direct-booking traffic to European businesses by 30%; test users showed high dissatisfaction) rest on data only Google holds, and travel-tech groups counter that a worse experience was Google's own design choice. For anyone with European clients, this is the first look at what regulated AI search looks like: fewer features, more intermediaries, and a company grading its own remedy.

### The question box became an answer box: 97% of People Also Ask results are AI Overviews

Fourteen months ago, about 12% of People Also Ask answers were AI-generated. In the first week of September it was 97%, per AlsoAsked founder Mark Williams-Cook's monthly series across 19.2 million English-language PAAs collected since January. The path wasn't smooth (22% in January, down to 11% in April, then 40%, 64%, 61%, 86%, 97%), and a second tracker, Allintitle's Also Ask Miner, reads it as a flat 100% since August. Williams-Cook declined that rounding when Barry Schwartz asked ("almost at 100% but not there yet") but added: "It seems highly likely we are on track for 100% shortly." The classification is Google's own: since late 2024 some PAA answers have carried an AI-generated label instead of an extracted passage credited to one page.

For content programs built on question-shaped queries, this changes what winning looks like. PAA used to be a second listing on page one for a page that couldn't crack the top three; now the box answers the question itself and your page becomes a citation under it, if it appears at all. Question content still gets read, inside Google. If your reporting still treats a PAA appearance as a traffic source instead of a visibility surface, it will show a decline that your actual exposure doesn't match. Google spent the same week saying positions one through ten are "hard to map" for site owners and testing thinner citation panels in AI Overviews.

### Amazon's DSP now sells ChatGPT ads. OpenAI closed the door on rival image and voice tools the same week.

From Wednesday, September 10, select US advertisers on Amazon DSP can extend campaigns into ChatGPT, with Delta Vacations first named. It's a managed service, bought on CPC or CPM, with product-feed ads that build creative from a catalog, and the split of control is the detail to hold onto: Amazon runs the buying and campaign layer, OpenAI decides where and whether the ads appear. Digiday's frame is the right one: it's the latest stop on an 18-month supply-chasing streak (Netflix, Roku, Spotify, SiriusXM, Disney, Hulu, ESPN), and it plugs OpenAI's inventory into an audience graph retail advertisers already work with. OpenAI told Adweek its ads business is on track to pass $1 billion over the next 12 months; Sensor Tower measured ads served per US user per hour up 163% between April and August; the reported internal target is $100 billion by 2030.

The other OpenAI story landed a day earlier and wasn't announced. Per The Information, OpenAI told advertising partners it will no longer approve campaigns for standalone image- or audio-generation products. Adobe, a February pilot participant that promoted Firefly, is affected; its media lead Doug Wyatt confirmed on LinkedIn that "image and voice/audio are closed off. Video's still open…for now." OpenAI shipped ChatGPT Images 2.5 the same week and launched its own voice product in July, and Adgully reports outbound referral links are being restricted for some image-editor queries. OpenAI's published policy simply reserves the right to refuse ads for any reason. Platforms blocking direct competitors is old behavior. The new part is a platform expanding into its advertisers' categories and setting the exclusions privately, which means anyone planning ChatGPT spend should check their product against a rulebook that can move without notice.

### Your Shopping CTR went up. Mike Ryan checked the denominator.

Across 175 billion impressions from thousands of Shopping and Performance Max campaigns in hundreds of accounts, Smarter Ecommerce's Mike Ryan found median Shopping impressions fell from roughly 1.85 million to 1.4 million between mid-2025 and mid-2026 while median click-through rate rose from 1.20% to nearly 1.55%. Clicks were flat or slightly down. Optmyzr's separate ecommerce data showed CTR up 17% year over year, close to the 20% Ryan saw in his own benchmarks. Publishers have spent two years living the "crocodile effect" (impressions up, clicks down) since AI Overviews launched; Shopping ads are showing the mirror image, and Ryan's hypothesis is that Google serves AI Overviews preferentially on queries with lower predicted ad-click probability, preserving Shopping placements where clicks are likely. He calls it a bridging strategy toward "both/and" experiences as Google scales Shopping ads inside the AI answers themselves. Google's documentation confirms ads can appear above, below, or within an Overview; it doesn't confirm predicted-CTR routing, and Ryan presents it as a theory.

The reporting consequence doesn't wait for the theory to be proven. A CTR improvement in a Q4 board pack that is really an impression decline will be read as a win by everyone who sees it. Put impression volume, clicks, and total traffic next to CTR, and rebaseline every Shopping benchmark set in 2025. Search Engine Watch added a data point the same week: on a headphones query in AI Mode, every product in the answer was an ad, no organic listing among them. One test, but it earns a monitoring line.

### Two datasets on what AI answers do to demand before you get a look in

Exploding Topics surveyed more than 2,300 US consumers about AI in their purchase journeys, and the headline finding is a negative one: 57.5% of AI users decided not to buy something based on what a chatbot told them. Almost two-thirds have at least partially replaced product-related Google searches with AI conversations, usage is rising over time, and more than four in ten say they actively dislike AI chatbot ads, which complicates the obvious response of buying your way back into the conversation. The unglamorous fix is the right one: the model talked the customer out of it using material it found somewhere (reviews, spec pages, comparison content, third-party write-ups), and most of that material is fixable by someone on your team this quarter.

On the paid side, SE Ranking published the numbers from its own ChatGPT Ads test instead of another explainer: three campaigns, eight ad groups, 48 ads across the US, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand in June, producing 97,131 impressions and 1,263 clicks, a 1.30% average CTR. Hold that next to the week's other ChatGPT Ads news: expansion into selected markets across Europe, India, the Middle East, and North Africa, plus a performance stack arriving fast (editable custom audiences, mixed identifiers, audiences above five million, Google Advertising ID support, and a Pixel and Conversions API that now accept phone numbers, names, regions, and postal codes). The infrastructure is running ahead of the incrementality evidence, which is normal for a new channel and a reason to keep the test budgets labeled as tests.

### Blogging confidence hit a twelve-year low. AI adoption hit 92%.

Just 14% of marketers say their blog delivers strong results, per Orbit Media's annual blogging survey: six points below the previous twelve-year low and roughly half the 26% reported in 2022. The explanation isn't the one you'd expect. AI adoption reached 92.4% and showed no relationship to results in either direction. What correlated with the decline was the abandonment of practices historically tied to success: influencer collaboration, original research, keyword research, paid promotion, formal human editing, and consistent use of analytics. Marketers who still did keyword research were more likely to report strong results.

AI did what it promised on speed. The average post now takes 3 hours and 20 minutes, down from more than four hours in 2022, saving a typical marketer about 50 hours of writing a year while reported performance kept sliding. MarketingProfs' Ann Handley called the findings a wake-up call about being more discerning about which efforts get removed and which get kept. Read alongside this week's other numbers (a question box that answers itself, a chatbot that talks shoppers out of purchases, a LinkedIn feed that penalizes generated-sounding posts), the survey describes a trade the industry made a year ago: speed replaced the parts of the process that made the output good, and this is what the trade looks like on the far side.
