## Act One — the lead story, and where most of the time should go: Uber's two-week experiment

Tell this as a story about people, not a product announcement.

Uber's Chief Technology Officer, Praveen Neppalli Naga, published one of the most concrete large-organization AI adoption reports anyone has shared, on July 7. Inside Uber's engineering organization, AI is already everywhere — by the company's own numbers, 99 percent of engineers use AI tools, more than 70 percent of pull requests are attributed to local or cloud agents, and engineers have created more than 2,500 agent skills. Those figures come from Uber itself; they are company-reported, and Uber is the only source for them — say that plainly.

But the interesting part is what Uber did next. The engineers were already converted; the rest of the company — finance, legal, operations, marketing, customer support, HR, procurement — was not. Uber's answer was a program called **Agentic Pods**: take roughly 30 AI-proficient engineers who know Uber's systems deeply, and pair each one with a domain expert from a business function. Then give each pair exactly two weeks. The day-by-day rhythm is the story, because the discipline of it is unusual:

- Days 1 and 2: the engineer shadows the expert — watches every step, documents the workflow, asks questions.
- Day 3: together they prioritize opportunities by scale, repetition, business impact, and data availability.
- Days 4 and 5: they build a working agent side by side with the person who actually does the job.
- Days 6 through 9: they validate it with additional people who do the same work.
- Day 10: they ship.

Uber ran 16 pods across 16 business functions in two months. The reported results are startling even one at a time:

- Capital allocation analysis across 150 cities went from 15 hours to 30 minutes.
- Financial pacing reports went from two days to 10 minutes.
- Marketing website quality assurance went from two weeks to 50 minutes.
- A support operation with 9,000 manually created workflows moved to self-service automation.

Here is the twist to draw out in discussion. Naga's biggest finding: the largest gains came from redesigning the workflow itself around AI — removing handoffs, cutting unnecessary approvals, replacing older tools, connecting work across systems. Speeding up individual tasks turned out to be the smaller effect. His own words, and the quote of the week:

> "The workflow becomes the unit of automation — not the individual task."

Here is the tension worth sitting with: why does the embedding matter? Because a task can be automated from a written instruction, but a workflow is full of things nobody writes down — exceptions, judgment calls, file locations, approval habits, unwritten conventions. Uber's model puts the technical builder next to the domain expert long enough to discover those. The parallel for an AV department is easy to see and worth stating: production work crosses intake, scheduling, asset preparation, recording, editing, review, captioning, metadata, publishing, archiving, and support — exactly the kind of multi-step, exception-heavy workflow Uber was targeting. Observe the parallel; leave the conclusions to the listeners.

## Act Two — the same shift shows up in everyday tools: GPT-5.6 goes GA

Bridge from Act One: what Uber built with embedded engineers, the big labs are now trying to package as a product.

On July 9, OpenAI moved the **GPT-5.6** model family from limited preview to general availability — the Dispatch covered the preview in its June 26 edition, so this is a follow-through, not a surprise. The family has three tiers: **Sol**, the flagship; **Terra**, the balanced model; and **Luna**, the lower-cost model. The change that matters is access: GPT-5.6 is now rolling out through ChatGPT, Codex, and the OpenAI API rather than being restricted to a small preview group.

Around the models, OpenAI shipped a set of orchestration features — an Ultra mode that coordinates multiple agents across parallel workstreams, Program Calling that lets the model write and run programs to coordinate tools, and a multi-agent capability entering beta in the API — all pointed at coordinating work rather than answering single prompts.

The piece that carries this act is **ChatGPT Work** — an agent designed to stay with a project for hours, act across connected applications and files, break a large goal into steps, and produce finished documents, spreadsheets, slides, and web applications. One way to see it, as an observation about direction rather than a claim of equivalence: ChatGPT Work looks like a packaged cousin of what Uber's pods hand-built — an agent that owns a workflow rather than a task. Then add the sober note the Dispatch always adds: for any agent working across organizational systems, the operational questions extend beyond benchmarks — which applications it can access, what permissions it receives, how its actions are reviewed, and what records remain afterward.

One more item belongs here: OpenAI also launched **GPT-Live** on July 8 — a new ChatGPT Voice model with a full-duplex architecture, meaning it listens and speaks at the same time, handles interruptions, and manages faster conversational turn-taking. GPT-Live-1 is rolling out to Go, Plus, and Pro users, a smaller version is becoming the Free default, and API access is planned. At launch, GPT-Live is voice-only; combining voice with video or screen sharing remains unavailable for now. For an audio-minded audience, full-duplex is a fun concept to explain — it is the difference between a walkie-talkie and a phone call.

## Act Three — media production's version of the platform shift: Runway Dev and Meta's Muse

Now bring it home to media tools, the audience's native territory.

**Runway Dev**, launched July 8, is Runway's answer to a question every production-adjacent developer now faces: which model do you build on when no single model is best at everything? Runway's answer is: don't choose — route. Runway Dev is a single enterprise platform and API offering Runway's own models — Gen-4.5, Aleph 2.0, and Act-Two — alongside third-party models including ByteDance Seedance, OpenAI GPT Image 2, and ElevenLabs models. Runway's pitch to developers is that switching between models takes a one-line code change — a claim that captures where media infrastructure is heading: individual models can change while the surrounding application, governance, and billing layer stays in place.

There are reusable "Recipes" for multi-step media workflows, one billing dashboard with per-model cost visibility, and a planned intelligent routing layer. Just as notable is what the platform competes on besides quality: contractual guarantees that customer data stays out of model training, zero-data-retention support, SOC 2 Type II compliance, IP indemnification, built-in content moderation, and a 99.9 percent uptime commitment. Those are vendor claims, straight from Runway's own materials — but they show that generative media infrastructure is now competing on data handling and governance, not just output quality.

Meanwhile, on July 7, Meta Superintelligence Labs shipped its first media models: **Muse Image**, available now, and **Muse Video**, in preview. Muse Image is worth a moment because it goes beyond a simple prompt-to-image system — Meta describes an agentic media model that follows detailed instructions, edits precisely, composes from multiple reference images, can use tools like web search and code execution, and reviews and refines its own output before returning a result. Meta also introduced **Content Seal**, an invisible provenance signal designed to remain detectable after an image is cropped, compressed, resized, or captured in a screenshot — with a detector in preview and plans to extend it to video. If it proves reliable, provenance that survives ordinary media handling would be more useful than metadata that disappears during export.

Give Meta credit on air for an unusual disclosure: Muse Video generates native audio, but Meta itself named the two things that still need work — audio-video synchronization, and physically accurate fast motion. Native audio is becoming a standard launch claim in video models, yet generating sound and picture together still leaves synchronization as an open problem. Keep that sync gap in mind — it returns when the conversation reaches the plumbing.

## Act Four — the plumbing nobody demos: LucidLink and AVTensor

This is the sleeper story: none of the stories above work without the unglamorous layer underneath.

**LucidLink** announced Connect for Frame.io on July 7. The problem it addresses is one this audience lives with: Frame.io handles Camera-to-Cloud footage, dailies, and review, but the supporting assets — graphics folders, audio libraries, archives, departmental storage — live everywhere else. LucidLink Connect presents Frame.io footage and those external assets inside one real-time filespace **without moving, migrating, or duplicating a single file**. Original storage paths and permissions stay intact, editors work in Adobe Premiere, DaVinci Resolve, or Avid Media Composer, and the system streams only the portions of a file needed for playback, scrubbing, or editing. It is currently available for enterprise accounts. The tie to the through-line: agents and media models can only work on assets they can actually reach — the data layer decides what automation is practical.

Then the callback to Meta's sync gap: Runway open-sourced **AVTensor** on July 10 — a Rust media decoder for training pipelines that returns audio and video tensors sharing the same time origin. Runway built it after discovering an existing decoder was silently misaligning audio and video in training data. In Runway's reported benchmarks, direct object-storage streaming cut a 100-batch decoding run from 176 seconds to 122 seconds and improved model flops utilization by 1.8 percentage points in a production training run. The elegant point: synchronization problems in AI video can begin before the model is ever trained — sometimes the fix is a better decoder, and anyone who has chased a lip-sync drift through a broadcast chain will feel that one.

End with the practical habit from this week's Tip of the Week, presented as information about a technique, with no directives to the audience. The Dispatch's "Back to Basics" series is on Week 7, and the habit is this: an AI learns a desired output format faster from two representative examples than from a long written description. Two good examples establish structure, tone, level of detail, and labeling at a glance. The pattern works best when confidential names and details are removed from the examples first, and when the model is told explicitly to produce entirely new content that follows only the structure and formatting of the examples.
