### The first outside data on August 17: budget-limited CPCs up 15.8%

Google updated its help page to confirm the August 17 bidding change finished rolling out globally on August 27, and Smarter Ecommerce's Mike Ryan posted the first outside read on Monday, September 14: median CPCs for budget-limited Target ROAS campaigns rose 15.8% after the change, while campaigns that were never budget-limited got 13% cheaper. His explanation is the useful part. Google had been lowering bids on budget-capped campaigns to keep them under budget, which left most of them "rank-limited by CPC"; ending that suppression pushed their CPCs up and released "a sizable pool of cheap clicks" to campaigns without a budget cap. On the PPC Live podcast he added a finding practitioners will recognize: advertisers raised tROAS targets around the update even on campaigns that weren't budget-limited, a reflex the change didn't call for. Conversion and ROAS reads still need one to two conversion cycles, which puts the fuller picture in mid-October.

**Comment window:** open until that read. The practical post: for budget-limited target campaigns, the lever moved from the target to the budget, because Google now holds performance at target when the budget changes.

### The ad-tech opinion is out: AdX into Prebid, AdWords out of DFP, a monitor for six years

The sealed opinion behind the September 2 order (our September 7 lead) came out Tuesday, unredacted because neither side asked for redactions, and it spells out what "behavioral remedies" means in practice. Structural relief was "neither realistic nor needed," Judge Leonie Brinkema wrote: the government hadn't shown the causal link a breakup requires, and DFP's auction logic wasn't part of the violation, while open-sourcing it would have been impractical (one DFP function depends on a daily computation that runs ten hours across 4,000 machines). What Google gets instead is a compliance build. AdX and DFP must integrate with Prebid, the open-source header-bidding framework, "in a functionally equivalent manner and on materially identical terms" to how DFP works with AdX today, and AdX has to bid into rival publisher ad servers on the same terms. Google must document how DFP picks a winner, share real-time bid data with publishers, and stay away from the First Look and Last Look mechanics the liability ruling condemned.

The buy side is where advertisers should look. The remedies apply "only as to AdWords, and not as to DV360": AdWords can't bid straight into DFP (the court flagged Google Partner Bidding as the route that would have sidestepped everything else), can't favor Google's own tools, and can't use first-party data to advantage Google's own pipes. Brinkema adopted Google's proposed six-year term over the government's 15, with a monitor and technical committee holding access to employees, systems, and source code. The Final Judgment takes effect 60 days after it's entered, and the joint draft is due in early October.

### Google will pay some publishers for shaping AI answers. Being linked doesn't count.

Digiday reported on Monday, September 14, that Google has been quietly scaling an invite-only "AI contribution pilot" in Search Console that pays websites when their content "contributes significantly to the creation of AI-generated responses" in the Gemini app, AI Overviews, and AI Mode. The eligibility line is the whole design. Payment covers the generation phase only: content that confirms a fact or gets linked after the answer exists doesn't qualify. Participants get an earnings widget with a monthly figure and some history, no explanation of how it's calculated, no upfront fee, and an opt-out at any time. Digiday's sources say dozens of publishers have been approached, well beyond news organizations. Google called it an early-stage learning pilot and pointed back to a June post about rewarding content that keeps generative answers fresh and accurate. Critics, in Digiday's summary, see it as "less like a meaningful payout and more like a legal fig leaf."

For brands with owned content programs, the incentive is now explicit even if the program isn't open to them yet: Google is putting a price on content that shapes what the model says, separate from the click. A link in an AI answer and a contribution to it are different assets, and only one has a payout attached. The widget shows a monthly total, not which pages earned it or why.

### Ads moved inside the answer: Sponsored Agents in ChatGPT, sponsored links in AI Mode

OpenAI said Wednesday it's testing Sponsored Agents with select US advertisers: after seeing a relevant ad, "a user can choose to start a clearly labeled conversation with a business-sponsored agent in ChatGPT," ask follow-up questions, and click through to the business when ready. OpenAI says that conversation is "distinct from ChatGPT's independent answers and separate from the original conversation." The same post turned ChatGPT into its own ad-building tool (campaigns created from prompts in ChatGPT Work, an Ads Manager plugin that creates, updates, and analyzes campaigns in plain language, creative drafted from the landing page) and named two partners. HubSpot, its first CRM integration, lets businesses connect a ChatGPT Ads account, run ads, and follow up on leads without leaving HubSpot; Shopify's US merchants got a ChatGPT Ads app backed by Shopify Catalog, going international September 23. The Register's summary was less charitable: "Slop ads served in slop responses."

Google spent the same days testing its version. Sachin Patel spotted, and Search Engine Roundtable reported, sponsored results inside AI Mode rendered as ordinary anchor-text links in the answer, with a "Sponsored" label above, the paid cousin of the organic anchor-text links Google first tested in AI Mode in April 2025. Barry Schwartz's doubt is the practical one: people click links inside AI answers less, so an ad dressed as a citation may win the placement and lose the click. Both formats move the ad from beside the answer into it, which puts disclosure design on the media plan next to bidding.

### Google's holiday playbook: share of voice in AI answers, and a way to take the checkout back

Ashish Gupta, who runs merchant shopping at Google, laid out the holiday agentic-commerce toolkit on Wednesday, September 16. AI performance insights in Merchant Center, which compares a retailer's share of voice with other brands across AI Mode and AI Overviews, is now generally available in the US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and India (English queries and organic AI traffic only, and still no click metric, per Search Engine Journal). Business Agent, the conversational layer that answers shopper questions, entered a US beta inside YouTube ads. And the Universal Commerce Protocol hub, which Google says already enables direct checkout for "hundreds of thousands" of brands, gained cart transfer: a shopper builds a cart on Google and finishes on the merchant's own site, where the merchant's upsells and account sign-ups still work. It's rolling out in the US now, with Australia and Canada early next year; analytics are "coming soon."

The feed guidance was the most concrete line in the post: in testing with lululemon, conversational attributes the brand submitted were used in 50% of relevant AI Mode product recommendations. Tapestry (Coach, Kate Spade) already sells through UCP checkout in Search, AI Mode, and Gemini. For retail clients, the product feed now writes part of the answer, and cart transfer is a concession that merchants might want the checkout back.

### Europe's remedy reached the shopping aisle: free listings gone, carousels down 90–100%

The DMA redesign Google shipped to the EEA on September 8 (our September 14 lead) kept spreading. By Friday, free product listings and the organic "popular products" carousels had largely disappeared from search across the EEA, replaced by a "Comparison Sites" aggregator unit that stacks price-comparison services with one expanded by default. Productrise's Hugo Huijer measured drops of roughly 90–100% in Germany, France, Belgium, Sweden, and the Netherlands, and Adriaan Dekker named where the space went: "The space is going to price comparison services (CSS) instead." Google's ads liaison Ginny Marvin confirmed the removal was a DMA requirement. The same day, Google added local business queries to the aggregator and supplier units: directories that win approval and send a local data feed compete for the aggregator slot, while the businesses themselves can appear in the companion supplier unit without sending anything extra, as long as Google can crawl their sites.

For European retail and local clients, this is a channel change more than a ranking change. Organic product visibility on Google in the EEA now runs through comparison services, which makes paid Shopping and CSS relationships the levers. Google has said the changes extend into AI Overviews and AI Mode there too.

### Microsoft's rules for AI-generated ads: disclose it, keep the watermark, and the label won't save a deepfake

Search Engine Land reported on Monday, September 14, that Microsoft Advertising has published dedicated guidance for AI-generated and AI-manipulated creative, and it reads like a production checklist. When law or policy requires disclosure, it has to be clear and close to the content, ideally built into the image or video itself. Advertisers must preserve watermarks, metadata, and provenance data, and interfering with machine-readable provenance is itself grounds for rejection. Microsoft's own AI tools embed imperceptible watermarks, which may not satisfy consumer-facing disclosure on their own. And the label doesn't launder the creative: ads can still be rejected, restricted, or removed for prohibited deepfakes, impersonation, or using someone's likeness or voice without the required authorization.

A week after an offshore sportsbook deepfaked a college football fan (last week's lead conversation), that last clause is the one brand teams should forward. The practical exposure sits in the export pipeline: any resize or re-encode step that strips metadata now creates policy risk on Microsoft's network, which moves provenance out of legal review and into production ops.
